10.3 Alignment: DPO vs. RLHF

Both teach a model to prefer one response over another. They disagree on how many moving parts that requires.

TL;DR
RLHF (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback) is a three-stage pipeline: supervised fine-tuning, then training a separate reward model on human preference pairs, then using reinforcement learning (typically PPO) to optimize the policy against that reward model. DPO (Direct Preference Optimization) collapses the last two stages into a single supervised loss — it trains directly on chosen/rejected response pairs, with no separate reward model and no RL loop at all. DPO is simpler, more stable, and the recommended default starting point for most teams; RLHF offers a higher performance ceiling for teams willing to invest in tuning it, particularly on tasks with complex or noisy preference signals.

Why DPO simplifies the pipeline so much

RLHF's reward model exists to convert messy human preference data into a single scalar reward signal, which reinforcement learning can then optimize against. DPO's key insight: that reward model is a middleman that can be removed entirely. Using a specific mathematical reformulation (built on the Bradley-Terry preference model), DPO derives a loss function that goes directly from preference pairs to policy updates — comparing how the current model and a frozen reference copy of it score the chosen versus rejected response, and adjusting so the preferred response becomes more likely. No reward model, no online generation loop, no PPO instability to debug.

Where RLHF's extra complexity still earns its keep

  • Noisy or complex preference signals. A learned reward model can generalize from noisy human preferences better than DPO's more direct optimization — the reward model acts as a denoising layer between raw, sometimes inconsistent preferences and the actual policy update.
  • Distribution shift during training. RLHF generates fresh responses from the current policy during training (on-policy data), which keeps the reward signal relevant even as the model's behavior meaningfully changes over the course of training — DPO trains on a fixed, pre-collected preference dataset instead.
  • Reusable reward model. Once trained, a reward model has other uses beyond the original RLHF run — as a standalone evaluator, a filter for synthetic data generation, or a scoring component in best-of-N sampling at inference time.

An honest, contested empirical picture

The original DPO paper reported it matching or exceeding PPO-based RLHF on tasks like sentiment control and summarization, while being simpler to train. But independent follow-up comparisons have found the outcome varies by task type, data quality, and model scale — some research specifically argues DPO underperforms reward-based RLHF on harder alignment tasks due to a limitation in how it implicitly represents reward. The practical, non-dogmatic takeaway used across current guides: start with DPO as the simpler, cheaper default; if it underperforms, the preference data quality or a distribution mismatch is the likely bottleneck — and that's the signal to consider investing in the added complexity of RLHF.

Check your understanding

1. What does DPO eliminate compared to standard RLHF?
Both DPO and RLHF still start from SFT and still require human preference data — DPO specifically removes the reward-model-training and RL-optimization stages that sit between the preference data and the final policy.
2. Why might RLHF's separate reward model help more than DPO on noisy preference data?
DPO optimizes more directly on the raw preference pairs, while RLHF's reward model sits in between and can smooth over noisy or inconsistent signal before it reaches the policy update step.
3. What's the recommended practical starting point according to current guidance?
This reflects the pragmatic, cost-conscious escalation pattern seen throughout this phase — try the simpler, cheaper, more stable option first, and only add complexity once there's real evidence it's needed.
