10.2 LoRA, QLoRA & PEFT

Training 1% of the weights, keeping 99% of the quality.

TL;DR
Full fine-tuning updates every one of a model's billions of weights — maximum flexibility, maximum GPU cost, and real risk of catastrophic forgetting (the model losing general capabilities while learning the new task). Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) freezes the base model and trains only a small set of additional parameters. LoRA is the dominant PEFT method — training roughly 0.1–1% of the original parameter count while matching full fine-tuning quality on most tasks. QLoRA goes further, quantizing the frozen base model to 4-bit precision while keeping the trainable adapters in higher precision — this is specifically what made fine-tuning 70B-class models practical on a single consumer-grade GPU.

How LoRA actually works, mechanically

Instead of updating a weight matrix directly, LoRA freezes it and adds a separate, much smaller pair of matrices (commonly called A and B) whose product approximates the change that full fine-tuning would have made. Two key hyperparameters control this: rank (the bottleneck width of these small matrices — typical values run 8 to 64) and alpha (a scaling factor for how strongly the adapter's output is applied). The result is a lightweight adapter — typically a few hundred megabytes, versus tens of gigabytes for the full model — that can be swapped in and out without touching the underlying frozen weights at all.

Why this matters beyond just saving GPU cost

  • Multiple adapters, one base model. Because the base model stays frozen and unchanged, you can train several different LoRA adapters (one per task, one per customer, one per style) and hot-swap between them at inference time without loading multiple full-sized models.
  • Lower catastrophic forgetting risk. Since the base weights never change, the model's general capabilities are preserved by construction — only the small adapter is learning the new, narrow behavior.
  • Faster iteration. A LoRA or QLoRA run on a well-curated dataset can complete in hours rather than days, which matters directly for the evaluate-iterate loop from Phase 9.

The QLoRA trade-off, honestly stated

Quantizing the base model to 4-bit precision isn't free — it can introduce a small quality loss compared to full-precision LoRA, and it shifts where computational bottlenecks appear during training. In practice, this trade-off is widely considered worth it: it's specifically what makes large-model fine-tuning accessible on hardware that would otherwise require multiple high-end GPUs working together.

Check your understanding

1. What fraction of a model's total parameters does LoRA typically train?
This small fraction is the core of PEFT's efficiency — a tiny set of added parameters captures most of the quality gain that updating every weight would produce, at a fraction of the compute.
2. What specifically does QLoRA add on top of standard LoRA?
The base model's precision is reduced to shrink its memory footprint dramatically, while the small trainable adapter matrices stay at higher precision to preserve training quality — this combination is what makes very large model fine-tuning fit on modest hardware.
3. Why does LoRA carry lower catastrophic forgetting risk than full fine-tuning?
Because the frozen base weights are structurally untouched, the model's general pre-trained capabilities are preserved by construction — the adapter adds new narrow behavior without overwriting what was already there.
