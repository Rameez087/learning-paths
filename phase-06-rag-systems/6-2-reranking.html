6.2 Reranking

A cheap first pass casts a wide net. A second, pricier pass decides what actually matters.

TL;DR
Initial vector retrieval uses a bi-encoder — it embeds the query and every document independently, then compares vectors, which is fast but approximate. A cross-encoder reranker takes each retrieved candidate together with the query, processes them jointly with full attention between the two, and produces a much more precise relevance score — at meaningfully higher cost per pair, which is exactly why it's applied only to a small pre-filtered candidate set, not the whole corpus. Reported production lifts are substantial: NDCG@10 improvements commonly in the 5–15 point range, 20+ on lexically hard datasets, for under 200ms of added latency.

Why bi-encoders and cross-encoders trade off differently

  • Bi-encoder (initial retrieval) — query and each document are embedded completely separately, ahead of time for documents, at query time for the query. This is what makes million-document vector search fast: documents are pre-embedded once, and search is just comparing the query vector against pre-computed document vectors.
  • Cross-encoder (reranking) — query and document are fed into the model together, letting attention operate across both simultaneously. This produces much more nuanced relevance judgments, but can't be precomputed — every query/document pair needs its own forward pass, so it doesn't scale to searching an entire corpus directly.

The standard production pattern combines both: retrieve a wide candidate set cheaply (say, top 20-50 via bi-encoder/hybrid search), then rerank just that candidate set with the expensive cross-encoder down to the final top 3-5 that actually go into the prompt.

The nuance the research surfaces: LLM-based reranking vs. cross-encoders

A 2026 systematic evaluation comparing lightweight cross-encoder rerankers against using an LLM itself as the reranker found a real trade-off, not a clean winner: cross-encoder rerankers offer meaningfully lower latency and cost, but showed a measurable decline in downstream answer faithfulness compared to LLM-based reranking in that study. The practical implication: "add a reranker" isn't a single decision — cheaper cross-encoders and pricier LLM-based reranking sit at different points on the same cost/quality curve, and the right choice depends on how much latency and cost budget the application actually has.

Check your understanding

1. Why can't a cross-encoder be used for the entire initial retrieval over a large corpus?
Bi-encoders can pre-embed every document once and reuse those vectors for every future query; a cross-encoder must jointly process the query and each specific document together, which doesn't scale to searching millions of documents directly.
2. What's the standard production pattern for combining retrieval and reranking?
This two-stage pattern is exactly why reranking is affordable in practice — it's applied to a pre-filtered handful of candidates (e.g. top 20-50), not the full corpus.
3. What did the 2026 research comparing cross-encoder and LLM-based reranking find?
The finding was a genuine trade-off, not a clean winner — cost/latency favored cross-encoders, faithfulness favored the LLM-based approach in that study, meaning the right choice depends on the specific application's constraints.
