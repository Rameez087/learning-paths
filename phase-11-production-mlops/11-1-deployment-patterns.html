11.1 Deployment patterns

A model can return a valid, successful HTTP response while being substantively wrong. That single fact breaks most traditional deployment playbooks.

TL;DR
LLM systems can fail silently: a candidate model or prompt change can return valid 200-status responses while quietly lowering task-completion rate or breaking output format. This makes naive "flip 100% of traffic and watch for errors" deployment dangerous for AI systems in a way it isn't for typical software. The standard progression: shadow (mirror real traffic to the candidate with zero user impact, compare offline) → canary (route a small, real percentage of live traffic, monitor quality metrics not just uptime) → gradual ramp to full rollout, with an explicit rollback trigger defined before you start, not improvised mid-incident.

Three patterns, three different jobs

  • Shadow / traffic mirroring — production requests get asynchronously copied to the candidate, which processes them without its response ever reaching a real user. Zero production risk, and specifically catches prompt or model regressions on the real request distribution before any user sees a candidate response. This is the cheap, mandatory first step most teams are warned not to skip.
  • Canary deployment — a small, real percentage of live traffic actually gets routed to the candidate. Unlike shadow, users are genuinely affected, so this needs faster detection and a tighter rollback path than shadow does. Route-level control matters for agentic systems specifically — you might canary only a single low-risk intent, or one enterprise tenant, rather than a flat percentage across everything.
  • Blue-green — two complete, identical environments; a full switch, not a gradual ramp. Best suited for near-zero-downtime infrastructure switches with simple state and an instant rollback need, rather than for gradually validating a change in model behavior.

The real incident pattern this is designed to prevent

A documented, illustrative shape of failure: a team completes a migration to a newer model. Offline evals look good, cost looks better, and the team flips 100% of traffic at once. By noon, a specific task-completion metric on one high-value agent has dropped measurably; by early afternoon, a refusal rate on a different agent has climbed. The root cause, on post-mortem: the offline evaluation set under-represented the real production traffic distribution, and there was no per-cohort live monitor or canary gate that would have caught the regression before it reached everyone. This is precisely the gap shadow and canary stages exist to close — offline evals are necessary, but not sufficient on their own.

Measure quality, not just uptime

A specifically flagged common mistake: measuring only HTTP success rate during a canary window. A model can return valid 200 responses while its task-completion rate quietly drops or its JSON output stops validating against the expected schema (3.2) — infrastructure-level health checks won't catch either. AI canary monitoring needs quality signals layered on top of infrastructure metrics: task completion, output validity, cost per request, and latency, tracked per cohort, not aggregated across a mixed population of very different users.

Check your understanding

1. Why is shadow deployment run before canary, rather than going straight to canary?
Shadow is the cost-bounded, zero-risk signal-gathering stage — it's cheap and catches many issues before spending the higher-stakes canary stage's "budget" of real user exposure.
2. Why can measuring only HTTP success rate during a canary window miss a real regression?
This is the core distinguishing risk of AI deployments versus typical software — a "successful" response at the infrastructure level says nothing about whether the actual content was correct or useful.
3. What went wrong in the illustrative incident described above, according to its post-mortem?
The failure wasn't a lack of testing — offline evals looked good — it was that offline testing alone can't substitute for a live, staged rollout with real quality monitoring at each stage.
