1.2 How LLMs actually work

No math. Just the mechanism — because everything else in this track depends on this being solid.

TL;DR
An LLM does one thing, over and over: given the text so far, it predicts a probability for every possible next token, picks one, appends it, and repeats. There's no lookup table, no stored "facts" in the way a database has facts — just a very good statistical guess at what comes next, refined by an architecture called the transformer, whose key trick is called attention: the ability for every word to look at every other word and decide how relevant it is.

Step 1: text becomes tokens

Before any prediction happens, your input text is broken into tokens — chunks that are often whole words, sometimes word pieces ("ing", "un-"), sometimes single characters for rare words. This isn't a detail to skim past: token count is literally what you pay for on every API call, and it's why a model can "run out of context" mid-conversation.

Each token is converted into a list of numbers — an embedding — that represents its meaning as a point in high-dimensional space. Words with similar meanings end up as nearby points. We'll go deeper on embeddings in Phase 5; for now, just know: token in, numbers out.

Step 2: attention — every token looks at every other token

This is the actual breakthrough, introduced in the 2017 paper that started the current era of AI. Before transformers, models processed text mostly in order, one word after another, which made it hard to connect a word to something said much earlier in a long passage.

Attention fixes this by letting every token directly examine every other token in the input, all at once, and assign each one a relevance score. Consider a sentence ending in "...therefore the murderer was" — to predict the next word correctly, the model needs the final token to have absorbed information from potentially the entire preceding passage: who had a motive, who was in the room, what clue was dropped three paragraphs back. Attention is the mechanism that lets that happen — it lets information flow from any token to any other token, weighted by relevance, regardless of distance between them.

Landmark paper
Attention Is All You Need — Vaswani et al., 2017. Introduced the transformer architecture and the self-attention mechanism described above, replacing the recurrent (step-by-step) architectures that came before it. Nearly every LLM in production today — GPT, Claude, Gemini, Llama — is a descendant of this paper's architecture. arxiv.org/abs/1706.03762

Step 3: predict, sample, repeat

After attention has let every token gather context from the others, the model produces a probability for every token in its entire vocabulary (tens of thousands of possibilities) being the next one. It then samples a token from that distribution — not always the single most likely one, which is where temperature comes in: a setting that controls how often the model picks a lower-probability token instead of the top guess. Temperature 0 is nearly deterministic; higher temperature means more variety, and occasionally more nonsense.

That new token gets appended to the sequence, and the entire process repeats: re-attend over everything so far (including the token you just generated), predict the next distribution, sample again. An LLM generating a 500-word answer is running this loop roughly 500+ times, each time re-examining the whole conversation so far.

What this explains about LLM behavior

  • Why LLMs "hallucinate": there's no fact-checking step. The model is producing the statistically plausible continuation of the text, and a confident-sounding wrong answer is exactly as "natural" a continuation as a correct one, if the training data made it look that way.
  • Why longer context costs more and gets slower: attention compares every token against every other token, so cost grows faster than the context length itself.
  • Why the same prompt can give different answers: sampling at nonzero temperature means the model isn't always picking the single most likely token.
  • Why models can "forget" something you said 50 messages ago: if it fell outside the context window, it's not in the token sequence being attended over at all — it isn't there to attend to.

Check your understanding

1. What does the attention mechanism actually do?
Attention is a relevance-weighting mechanism between tokens, not a storage or compression system — it's what lets a model connect "was" at the end of a paragraph back to a clue mentioned much earlier.
2. Why do LLMs sometimes generate confident, plausible-sounding wrong answers?
There's no fact-checking step in the generation loop — the model produces whatever continuation looks statistically plausible given its training, whether or not that continuation is true.
3. Setting temperature to 0 does what?
Temperature controls sampling randomness. At 0, the model consistently picks the top-probability token, making output close to deterministic; higher temperatures introduce more variety by occasionally picking lower-probability tokens.
