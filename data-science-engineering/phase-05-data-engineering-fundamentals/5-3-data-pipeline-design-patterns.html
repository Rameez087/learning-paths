5.3 Data pipeline design patterns

A pipeline that works once is easy to build. One that survives 3am failures, schema changes, and years of maintenance needs an actual architecture.

TL;DR
Beyond the batch/streaming choice (5.2), production pipeline architecture has a handful of named patterns worth knowing by name: Lambda architecture (parallel batch and speed layers for both accuracy and low latency), Kappa architecture (a single unified streaming-first pipeline, simpler but less mature for pure historical reprocessing), and event-driven/microservices patterns for complex, decoupled systems. The right pattern depends on data volume, latency needs, accuracy requirements, and how much operational complexity your team can actually sustain — not on which pattern sounds most sophisticated.

Lambda: accuracy and speed, at the cost of running two systems

Lambda architecture runs a batch layer (accurate, complete, but slower) and a speed layer (fast, approximate, low-latency) in parallel against the same incoming data, then merges their outputs — the batch layer's periodic accurate recomputation eventually corrects anything the speed layer got approximately right in the moment. The trade-off is explicit: you're maintaining two separate processing paths with two separate codebases, which is real, ongoing operational overhead.

Kappa: one pipeline, streaming-first

Kappa architecture's core simplification: treat everything as a stream, including reprocessing historical data (by replaying the stream from an earlier point) rather than maintaining a completely separate batch system. This removes Lambda's dual-codebase problem, but depends on your streaming infrastructure being mature enough to handle full historical reprocessing efficiently — not every streaming platform is built for that at scale.

Change Data Capture: a pattern specifically worth knowing by name

Change Data Capture (CDC) tools like Debezium track row-level changes (inserts, updates, deletes) directly from a source database's transaction log, streaming just the changes rather than periodically re-extracting the entire dataset. This is described as a more sustainable approach than periodic full extracts specifically because source systems are optimized for operational workloads (small, frequent queries), not the large analytical scans a full re-extract requires — CDC avoids putting that analytical load on a production database at all.

Dead letter queues: what happens to the record that fails

In any real pipeline, some records will fail to process — malformed data, an unexpected schema variant, a transient downstream error. A dead letter queue is a dedicated holding area for these failed records, so a single bad record doesn't halt the entire pipeline or get silently dropped. This is a deliberate design choice: fail loudly and recoverably for the bad record, keep processing everything else.

Check your understanding

1. What is the core trade-off of Lambda architecture?
Running a batch layer and a speed layer in parallel genuinely doubles the operational surface area — this real, ongoing cost is the direct trade for getting both low latency and eventual accuracy.
2. What does Kappa architecture simplify compared to Lambda?
The core simplification is treating reprocessing as "replay the stream" rather than maintaining an entirely separate batch codebase — this depends on the streaming infrastructure being mature enough for that role.
3. Why is CDC generally preferred over periodic full extracts from a production database?
CDC reads from the transaction log incrementally rather than scanning the whole table, which is specifically gentler on a production system built for small frequent queries rather than bulk analytical reads.
