5.2 Batch vs. streaming

Freshness is never free. Every hour you shave off latency costs real operational complexity.

TL;DR
Batch processes data in scheduled chunks — simple, cost-effective, and perfect when consumers can tolerate delay. Streaming processes events continuously with minimal delay — necessary when freshness itself is the requirement, but it demands strict duplicate handling, stable ordering, and genuinely more operational complexity. Micro-batching sits between the two: small batches processed frequently, approximating real-time behavior with a simpler execution model than full streaming. Most production data platforms in 2026 run a hybrid of all three, not one exclusively.

Why streaming isn't just "batch, but faster"

Streaming treats the dataset as fundamentally unbounded — there's no defined "end" to wait for, so state has to be managed through offsets, checkpoints, and time windows rather than a clean start-to-finish job. This introduces real operational demands that batch mostly avoids: strict handling of duplicate events, stable ordering guarantees, and managing backpressure (what happens when data arrives faster than it can be processed) and consumer lag (how far behind a consumer has fallen). None of this is optional complexity — it's what makes "continuous and low-latency" actually reliable rather than just fast and fragile.

Change Data Capture (CDC): a distinct third pattern

CDC specifically watches a source database's transaction log and captures only the rows that actually changed, rather than re-reading the entire table on every run (batch) or requiring the source to emit events itself (streaming). This is the standard pattern for keeping a downstream copy of a database in sync efficiently — syncing a 100GB database that only changes 1% daily via full batch reload would mean repeatedly moving 100GB for a 1GB change; CDC (via tools like Debezium) moves only the actual delta.

The concrete cost case for incremental over full-reload

As data volume grows, a full-reload batch job's processing time and cost grow roughly proportionally with total table size — reprocessing everything, every time, whether or not most of it changed. Incremental patterns (CDC, or batch jobs scoped to just new/changed records) keep processing cost roughly constant regardless of how large the total table gets. Reported case studies describe cost reductions of 50% or more from migrating full-reload batch jobs to incremental architectures, even as data volumes grew by an order of magnitude.

Check your understanding

1. Why does streaming require stricter duplicate and ordering handling than batch processing?
The unbounded, continuous nature of streaming means there's no natural "restart clean" point the way a batch job has — this structural difference is what forces stricter handling of duplicates, ordering, and retries.
2. What specifically does CDC capture that a full batch reload doesn't optimize for?
CDC's efficiency comes specifically from targeting the delta via the transaction log — this is why it dramatically outperforms full reloads for large tables with small daily change rates.
3. Why does incremental processing keep costs roughly constant as data volume grows, while full-reload batch doesn't?
This scaling difference is exactly why the reported 50%+ cost reductions occurred even as data volumes grew substantially — the cost driver (total table size) was removed from the processing cost equation.
