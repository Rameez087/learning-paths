5.4 Data ingestion strategies

How data actually gets from a source system into your pipeline in the first place.

TL;DR
Three main ingestion patterns, each fitting a different need: batch ingestion (pull everything accumulated on a schedule — simple, works well for reporting), CDC (Change Data Capture) (stream only row-level changes from a source's transaction log — efficient, low-impact on the source system), and streaming ingestion (continuous, event-by-event, near-real-time). The right choice depends on how fresh the data genuinely needs to be, not on defaulting to whichever sounds most modern.

Try it: pick the ingestion pattern for each scenario

Batch ingestion: the delivery truck

Batch ingestion collects everything that's accumulated since the last run and moves it all at once, on a schedule — hourly, daily, weekly. It's the simplest pattern to build, easiest to debug, and often the most cost-effective choice for reporting and analytics workloads that genuinely don't need near-real-time freshness. The instinct to default to something fancier "because streaming is more modern" is worth resisting when batch already meets the actual business need.

CDC: the conveyor belt for just the changes

Rather than periodically re-extracting an entire table (expensive and slow at scale), CDC tracks inserts, updates, and deletes directly from the source database's transaction log and streams just those changes. This is specifically valuable because production databases are tuned for small, frequent operational queries — a full-table extract runs against exactly the kind of large analytical scan those systems weren't optimized for, and CDC avoids putting that load on them at all.

Streaming: continuous, event-by-event

Streaming ingestion processes each event individually and continuously as it arrives, with latency measured in seconds rather than hours. This is the right fit when a decision genuinely needs to be made close to real time — fraud detection, live dashboards, alerting — but it comes with real added complexity: message ordering, exactly-once vs. at-least-once delivery guarantees, and state management for anything that needs to track values across events (like sessionization from 2.5, now happening as data arrives rather than after the fact in SQL).

At-least-once vs. exactly-once: a practical resolution

True exactly-once delivery is genuinely hard to guarantee in a distributed system. In practice, the dominant, pragmatic pattern is at-least-once delivery combined with idempotent consumers (5.5) — a message might occasionally be delivered more than once, but if processing that message twice produces the same result as processing it once, the duplication is harmless. This is simpler to build than true exactly-once semantics and nearly as effective in practice.

Check your understanding

1. Why might batch ingestion still be the right choice even in 2026, rather than defaulting to streaming?
Matching the ingestion pattern to the actual freshness requirement — not defaulting to whichever sounds more modern — is the practical lesson here, echoing the "start simple" theme from earlier phases.
2. Why does CDC put less load on a production database than a periodic full-table extract?
A full extract is a large analytical scan against a system built for small operational queries — CDC's incremental, log-based approach avoids that mismatch entirely.
3. Why is "at-least-once delivery + idempotent consumers" the dominant practical pattern over true exactly-once delivery?
This is a pragmatic engineering trade-off — rather than solving the genuinely hard exactly-once problem at the delivery layer, idempotency (5.5) solves it at the processing layer, which is simpler and nearly as effective.
