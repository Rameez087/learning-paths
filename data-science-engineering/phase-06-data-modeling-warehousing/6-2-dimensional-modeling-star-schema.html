6.2 Dimensional modeling & star schema

Every number in your business is either a measurement or the context around one. Dimensional modeling is just taking that split seriously.

TL;DR
Dimensional modeling, developed by Ralph Kimball, organizes a warehouse around exactly two table types: fact tables hold the quantitative, measurable events of the business (revenue, quantity, duration — numbers that grow into the billions of rows) and dimension tables hold the descriptive context around those measurements (who, what, where, when). A star schema — one central fact table directly joined to several dimension tables — is the simplest, most common form, and outperforms more normalized alternatives on roughly 90% of real analytical query workloads specifically because it needs fewer joins.

The golden rule

A fact table contains the numbers your business actually measures — revenue amount, quantity sold, duration, clicks. A dimension table describes the context those numbers happened in — the customer, the product, the store, the date. A useful test when you're unsure: if the column is something you'd sum, average, or count, it's probably a fact; if it's something you'd group by or filter on, it's probably a dimension.

Star vs. snowflake: the trade-off, in one sentence

A star schema keeps dimension tables flat and denormalized — everything about a product lives in one dim_product table, even if that duplicates some category information. A snowflake schema normalizes those dimensions further, splitting dim_product into separate linked tables for product, category, and subcategory. Star schemas win on roughly 90% of query workloads specifically because fewer joins means faster queries — snowflake schemas trade that query speed for reduced storage redundancy, which matters far less than it used to now that storage is cheap. Four types of fact tables worth knowing

  • Transactional — one row per business event (a single sale, a single click). The most common type.
  • Periodic snapshot — one row per defined time interval capturing a state (daily account balance).
  • Accumulating snapshot — one row per process instance, updated as it moves through stages (an order's full fulfillment lifecycle).
  • Factless — records that an event happened with no numeric measure attached (a student attended a class) — useful for counting occurrences.

Check your understanding

1. What's the simplest test for whether a column belongs in a fact table or a dimension table?
This distinction maps directly onto how the column would actually be used in a query — aggregated (fact) versus used for grouping/filtering (dimension).
2. Why do star schemas outperform snowflake schemas on most real analytical workloads?
The performance difference comes directly from join count — fewer joins in a star schema's flatter dimension tables generally means faster query execution, which is why it wins on the large majority of real workloads.
3. What makes a "factless" fact table useful despite having no numeric measure?
Sometimes the interesting question is simply "did this happen, and how often" rather than "how much" — a factless fact table captures exactly that, with the counting happening via COUNT(*) rather than SUM() on a measure column.
