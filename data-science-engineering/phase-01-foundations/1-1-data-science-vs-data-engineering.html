1.1 Data science vs. data engineering vs. analytics engineering

Same raw material, four different jobs — and the boundaries are blurrier in 2026 than the job titles suggest.

TL;DR
Four roles work with the same underlying data, at different points in its life: data engineers build the pipelines and infrastructure that move raw data somewhere usable; analytics engineers sit just downstream, transforming that raw data into clean, modeled tables business users can trust; data analysts interpret that modeled data into reports and dashboards; data scientists go further, building predictive models and statistical analyses. In 2026, tool overlap has blurred these lines significantly — a data analyst now often writes Python, a data engineer often builds ML pipelines — but the underlying purpose of each role has stayed genuinely distinct.

The data lifecycle, as a chain of custody

Thinking of these roles as stages in a pipeline clarifies the boundaries more than any job description: raw data is generated by applications and systems → a data engineer extracts it and lands it somewhere queryable (a warehouse or lake) → an analytics engineer transforms and models it into clean, trustworthy tables → a data analyst queries those tables to answer specific business questions and build dashboards → a data scientist uses the same modeled data (or sometimes raw data directly) to build predictive models and run deeper statistical analysis. Each stage depends on the one before it actually working.

Why analytics engineer is worth knowing as a distinct title

This is a genuinely newer role that emerged specifically to fill a gap: data engineers were spending too much time on business logic and data modeling (arguably not their core strength), while analysts were writing duplicated, inconsistent transformation logic across every dashboard. The analytics engineer role — closely associated with the rise of tools like dbt — owns the transformation layer specifically: turning raw, landed data into clean, tested, documented, reusable tables that both analysts and data scientists can trust as a single source of truth.

Why a mis-scoped hire is expensive

Industry hiring guidance is specific about the cost of getting this wrong: a mis-scoped senior data hire can cost 2-3x salary to unpick once you realize the person hired to do data science is actually needed for data engineering work, or vice versa. This is exactly why understanding the boundaries — even as they blur — matters practically, not just academically: it's the difference between hiring (or becoming) the right fit the first time.

Where this track focuses

This track covers the full chain — SQL and data manipulation (Phase 2), statistics (Phase 3), EDA and visualization (Phase 4), data engineering fundamentals (Phase 5), warehousing (Phase 6), orchestration (Phase 7), big data (Phase 8), and machine learning fundamentals through evaluation (Phases 9-10) — because in practice, especially at smaller companies or earlier in a career, these roles overlap constantly, and understanding the full chain makes you dramatically more effective at whichever piece you end up owning.

Check your understanding

1. What specific gap did the analytics engineer role emerge to fill?
The role specifically owns the transformation/modeling layer between raw landed data and business-ready tables — a responsibility that was previously split inconsistently between data engineers and analysts.
2. In the data lifecycle chain, what does a data engineer's output typically feed into?
Data engineers land raw data somewhere queryable; that raw data then typically gets transformed and modeled by an analytics engineer before analysts or data scientists work with it.
3. Why might a mis-scoped data hire cost significantly more to fix than to get right initially?
Despite blurring tool usage, the underlying job purposes remain genuinely distinct — hiring for the wrong one means the work that actually needed doing still isn't getting done, on top of the cost of a second search.
