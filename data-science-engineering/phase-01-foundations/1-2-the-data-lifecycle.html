1.2 The data lifecycle

Every dashboard, model, and report you've ever seen traveled this exact path first.

TL;DR
Data moves through five stages before it becomes a decision: generation (an app, sensor, or system produces it), ingestion (it's extracted and moved somewhere central), storage (it lands in a warehouse or lake), transformation (raw data becomes clean, modeled tables), and consumption (dashboards, reports, or ML models use it). This entire track is organized around this lifecycle — later phases go deep on each stage in order.

Why ELT won over ETL for most analytical workloads

A long-running architectural debate has largely settled: modern practice loads raw data directly into a powerful warehouse first, then transforms it there (ELT — Extract, Load, Transform) rather than transforming it in a separate step before loading (ETL — Extract, Transform, Load). This shift happened because cloud warehouses (Snowflake, BigQuery) became powerful and cheap enough to handle transformation workloads directly, and keeping the raw, untransformed data around turned out to be valuable — it means you can always re-derive a new transformation later without re-extracting from the original source. We'll cover this distinction in full in Phase 5.

Where each later phase of this track fits

  • Generation & ingestion — Phase 5 (data engineering fundamentals), Phase 7 (orchestration)
  • Storage — Phase 6 (data modeling & warehousing), Phase 8 (big data & distributed processing)
  • Transformation — Phase 2 (SQL), Phase 6 (warehousing/modeling)
  • Consumption — Phase 4 (EDA & visualization), Phase 9-10 (machine learning)
  • Cutting across every stage — Phase 11 (data quality, governance & observability) — because a broken stage anywhere in this chain silently corrupts everything downstream of it.

Check your understanding

1. What are the five stages of the data lifecycle, in order?
Data has to exist and be moved before it can be stored, and has to be stored before it can be cleaned up and modeled, and modeled before it's useful for a dashboard or model — the order reflects real dependency, not an arbitrary list.
2. Why did ELT become the dominant approach over ETL for analytical workloads?
The shift was driven by warehouse compute becoming cheap and powerful, plus the practical value of retaining raw untransformed data as a flexible source for future transformations.
3. Why does data quality/observability need to span the entire lifecycle rather than living in one stage?
Because each stage depends on the one before it, an issue introduced early (bad ingestion, a broken transformation) flows forward invisibly unless something is actively checking at every stage, not just the end.
