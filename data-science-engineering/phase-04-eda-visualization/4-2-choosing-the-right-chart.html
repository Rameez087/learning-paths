4.2 Choosing the right chart

The chart type isn't a style choice — it's a claim about what kind of comparison you're asking the viewer to make.

TL;DR
Chart selection should follow directly from your goal, not from habit or what looks impressive: comparing categories → bar chart; trend over time → line chart; relationship between two numeric variables → scatter plot; distribution/spread → histogram or box plot; composition (parts of a whole) → stacked bar, rarely pie. Every chart type has a specific failure mode when misused — a bar chart with too many categories becomes unreadable clutter; a line chart implies continuity even for data points that aren't actually continuous.

Why each chart type has a real failure mode, not just a "better fit"

  • Bar charts — excellent for comparing quantities across categories, but become cluttered and unreadable past roughly 8-10 categories, and can visually mislead if the axis doesn't start at zero (a small real difference gets visually exaggerated).
  • Line charts — good for continuous trends, but misleading with too many overlapping lines, and implicitly claim continuity between points — using one to connect genuinely discrete, unordered categories misrepresents the data's actual structure.
  • Pie charts — humans are measurably worse at comparing angles and areas than comparing bar lengths, which is why pie charts are widely discouraged for anything beyond 2-3 slices with clearly different sizes.
  • Scatter plots — reveal relationships bar and line charts can't, but with very large datasets, overlapping points (overplotting) can hide the true density of common values.

The axis-truncation trap

A documented, specific example: comparing donor rate trends between two countries where one line's x-axis quietly starts several years later than the other (due to missing early data), and the y-axis ranges differ between the two charts. The visual impression this creates — that the two regions have similar donor rates — is simply wrong once the axes are made comparable; the corrected version makes clear one region's rate is substantially higher. This is exactly why comparability of axes matters far beyond just this one example — it's a principle that resurfaces constantly in EDA work.

The data-ink ratio principle

Every visual element in a chart should earn its place. Heavy gridlines, unnecessary borders, redundant labels, and decorative color all compete with the actual data for the viewer's attention. The practical test: if removing an element doesn't lose any information, remove it — a cluttered chart with high "decoration" and low "data-ink" is measurably harder to read correctly than a clean one carrying the same information.

Check your understanding

1. Why do pie charts fall out of favor for anything beyond 2-3 clearly different slices?
This is a documented perceptual limitation, not an arbitrary style preference — bar length comparisons are simply easier and more accurate for human viewers than angle or area comparisons.
2. What made the donor-rate line chart example misleading before the axes were corrected?
The data itself wasn't wrong — the axis choices created a false visual impression of similarity that comparable axes immediately corrected, without changing a single underlying number.
3. What does the data-ink ratio principle recommend?
The test is specifically whether removing an element loses information — if not, it's decoration competing with the data itself for the viewer's limited attention.
