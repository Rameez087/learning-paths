4.1 The EDA workflow

"The greatest value of a picture is when it forces us to notice what we never expected." — John Tukey, who invented the discipline.

TL;DR
Exploratory Data Analysis is the bridge between raw data and any model or decision built on it — gather, tidy, visualize, analyze, interpret, in that order. Skipping straight to modeling without it is a specifically named, common failure: it produces models that don't accurately represent the data, because nobody checked what the data actually looked like first. EDA is fundamentally exploratory, not confirmatory — its job is to reduce uncertainty about what's in the data, not to confirm what you already assumed.

Try it: spot the real cause behind each EDA mistake

The five-stage loop

  1. Gather — assemble the data, understanding where it came from and what it's supposed to represent.
  2. Tidy — remove duplicates, handle missing values, fix obvious errors, before drawing a single chart.
  3. Visualize — plot distributions and relationships to surface patterns a table of numbers hides.
  4. Analyze — apply the statistical measures from Phase 3 (mean, spread, correlation) to what the visuals surfaced.
  5. Interpret — turn patterns into an actionable finding, not just a chart.

This is a loop, not a straight line — a pattern found in step 3 often sends you back to step 2 (is this an outlier, or a data-entry error I need to fix?), and this back-and-forth is normal, expected behavior, not a sign you're doing it wrong.

Exploratory vs. explanatory: know which mode you're in

This distinction is described as the single most common cause of ineffective visualizations — presenting a raw exploratory dump to an audience that actually needed a refined summary. During exploratory analysis, you're the only audience: crack open every chart quickly, keep what's useful, discard the rest, no need for polish. During explanatory communication (3.5's capstone-facing dashboards, a report to stakeholders), only the "pearls" — the one or two charts that actually carry the finding — should ever reach an audience, polished and clearly annotated. Publishing a dense, cluttered exploratory plot as a final deliverable is a specifically named, common mistake.

EDA should reduce uncertainty, not manufacture false confidence

A single compelling chart can be misleading on its own — real practice cross-checks any visual impression against the statistics from Phase 3 and against relevant subgroup comparisons, rather than trusting one plot in isolation. This connects directly to the correlation-vs- confound issue: an apparent relationship in a chart may be driven by a confound, duplicated records, or sampling bias rather than the causal story that seems obvious at first glance.

Check your understanding

1. Why is skipping EDA before modeling specifically flagged as a common, costly mistake?
EDA surfaces data quality issues, outliers, and unexpected patterns before they silently corrupt a downstream model — skipping it means those issues go undetected until much later, if at all.
2. What's the key difference between exploratory and explanatory visualization?
Mixing these up — presenting a raw exploratory dump to a stakeholder audience — is specifically named as the single most common cause of ineffective visualizations.
3. Why shouldn't a single compelling chart be trusted on its own?
Cross-checking a visual impression against statistics and subgroup comparisons is what prevents EDA from manufacturing false confidence out of what might just be a spurious pattern.
