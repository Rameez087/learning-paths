4.4 Visualization pitfalls

The exact same numbers can look like a crisis or a non-event, depending entirely on where the y-axis starts.

TL;DR
The most common chart manipulations don't require faking any numbers — they exploit how the human visual system perceives scale and proportion. A truncated y-axis (not starting at zero) can turn a 2% difference into something that visually looks like a 200% difference. This isn't always malicious — sometimes it's a genuine judgment call for showing small but real variation — but it demands a label and an explicit reason, not silent default behavior.

The catalog of common pitfalls

  • Truncated axis — as shown above. Always ask whether starting at zero would tell a meaningfully different story.
  • Dual y-axes with different scales — plotting two unrelated series on separate y-axes on the same chart can create a visual correlation that doesn't exist in the underlying numbers, purely from how the two scales happen to line up.
  • Cherry-picked time windows — a metric can look like it's trending up or down depending entirely on where the window starts and ends; the same underlying data can support opposite-looking stories.
  • 3D charts — perspective distortion in 3D bar or pie charts makes elements in the back visually appear smaller than equal-sized elements in front, misrepresenting the actual values.
  • Inconsistent bin sizes in histograms — uneven bins can make a distribution look smoother or spikier than the data actually is.

When truncation is legitimate — and how to handle it responsibly

Sometimes a genuinely important signal (a small but real change in a tightly-clustered metric, like a fever chart tracking small temperature changes) would be invisible on a zero-based axis. The responsible approach in this case: truncate the axis if it's genuinely warranted, but label it clearly and explicitly — a visible break mark on the axis, or a caption stating the axis doesn't start at zero — so the viewer isn't misled about the true scale of difference being shown.

Simpson's paradox: when the aggregate lies

A famous, real pattern: a trend can appear in several different groups of data but reverse or disappear when the groups are combined — or vice versa. This happens when a hidden variable (like relative group sizes) is unevenly distributed across the subgroups. This is exactly why the EDA principle from 4.1 — cross-check any aggregate visual against subgroup comparisons — matters as more than just good practice; skipping it can mean drawing precisely the wrong conclusion from technically accurate data.

Check your understanding

1. Why can a truncated y-axis make a small real difference look dramatic?
The numbers stay identical — only the visual proportion changes, which is exactly why axis truncation is a perceptual manipulation rather than a data manipulation.
2. What should accompany a genuinely justified truncated axis?
The distinction between legitimate and misleading truncation isn't whether you truncate — it's whether you disclose it clearly enough that the viewer can correctly interpret what they're seeing.
3. What causes Simpson's paradox?
This is a real statistical phenomenon, not an error — an uneven hidden variable across subgroups can make the combined trend point in a different direction than every individual subgroup, which is exactly why subgroup cross-checking matters.
