10.3 Evaluation metrics

A model that predicts "not fraud" for every single transaction can hit 99.9% accuracy — and be completely useless.

TL;DR
Accuracy alone is dangerously misleading on imbalanced data — a model that always predicts the majority class can score deceptively high while being practically useless. Precision answers "of everything flagged positive, how much actually was?" Recall answers "of everything actually positive, how much did we catch?" F1 balances the two. For ranking or probability-based models, use ROC-AUC, or better, PR-AUC specifically when positive cases are rare, since ROC curves can look artificially good under severe class imbalance.

Precision vs. recall: the trade-off, concretely

In fraud detection, high recall (catch nearly every fraud case) usually comes at the cost of lower precision (more false alarms — legitimate transactions incorrectly flagged). Which matters more depends entirely on the cost of each error type: if missing real fraud is far more costly than a false alarm's customer friction, prioritize recall; if false alarms erode customer trust badly, prioritize precision. There's no universally "better" choice — it's a business decision the metric alone can't make for you.

Why PR-AUC beats ROC-AUC for rare positive classes

ROC curves plot true positive rate against false positive rate — but with severe class imbalance, the false positive rate can stay deceptively low even when the model performs poorly at actually distinguishing rare positives, because there are just so many negatives to compare against. Precision-Recall AUC is specifically recommended instead when positive cases are rare, since it directly measures performance on the class that actually matters, without the imbalance-driven distortion ROC-AUC is prone to.

Calibration: does "80% confident" actually mean 80%?

Beyond just ranking predictions correctly, calibration checks whether predicted probabilities match real-world frequencies — if a model says "80% probability of fraud" for a batch of transactions, roughly 80% of them should actually turn out to be fraud. This matters specifically anywhere the probability value itself gets used downstream (risk scoring, automated decision thresholds), not just the final yes/no classification.

Check your understanding

1. Why can a model that always predicts the majority class achieve high accuracy while being practically useless?
This is the accuracy paradox — accuracy simply measures overall correct predictions, which a majority-class-only model can dominate on imbalanced data without ever correctly identifying a single rare positive case.
2. What determines whether you should prioritize precision or recall in a given application?
This is a business decision, not a purely technical one — the metric only tells you the trade-off exists; deciding which side to favor requires knowing which error type actually costs more in that specific context.
3. Why is PR-AUC often preferred over ROC-AUC when positive cases are rare?
The imbalance-driven distortion in ROC-AUC comes specifically from the abundance of true negatives making the false positive rate look artificially small — PR-AUC avoids this by focusing entirely on precision and recall for the positive class.
