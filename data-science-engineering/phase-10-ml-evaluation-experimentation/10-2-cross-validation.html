10.2 Cross-validation

"Cross-validation is not just a statistical ritual — it's your model's reality check." One split can get lucky. Five splits rarely all do.

TL;DR
K-fold cross-validation splits the data into k equal parts ("folds"), trains and evaluates the model k times — each time holding out a different fold as the test set — and averages the results. This gives a far more reliable performance estimate than a single train/test split, which can look misleadingly good or bad purely from how the data happened to be divided. Watching the variance across folds, not just the average, matters too — a high standard deviation across folds is a warning that the model might not generalize well.

Stratified K-Fold for classification

Just as with a simple train/test split (10.1), plain K-Fold can create folds with very different class balances purely by chance — especially damaging with imbalanced data. StratifiedKFold ensures each fold preserves the overall class proportions, and is the standard recommendation for classification tasks, while plain KFold remains appropriate for regression, where there's no class balance to preserve.

Choosing k: a real trade-off, not an arbitrary number

A commonly cited choice is k=5, corresponding to an 80/20 train/test split per fold — a larger holdout per fold (like k=5) is less prone to overfitting the hyperparameter search than a smaller holdout (like k=10, a 90/10 split), though other values of k can be justified based on the specific dataset and computational constraints. There's no single universally correct k; it's a documented trade-off between validation-set size and computational cost.

What high fold-to-fold variance actually tells you

If accuracy swings from 0.95 on one fold to 0.72 on another, that's not noise to ignore — it's a direct signal the model's performance depends heavily on which specific data it happened to see, meaning it likely won't generalize reliably to new, unseen data. This is exactly the kind of diagnostic information a single train/test split would never reveal, since it only ever shows you one particular split's result.

Check your understanding

1. Why does k-fold cross-validation give a more reliable performance estimate than a single train/test split?
A single split's result depends on how that particular division happened to fall — averaging across multiple different divisions smooths out this luck-of-the-split effect.
2. Why is StratifiedKFold generally preferred over plain KFold for classification tasks?
Without stratification, a fold could randomly end up with very few examples of a rare class, giving an unreliable performance read for that fold — stratification directly guards against this.
3. What does high variance in scores across cross-validation folds indicate?
A model that performs wildly differently depending on which fold it saw is exhibiting instability that a single average score would hide entirely — this is exactly why watching variance, not just the mean, matters.
