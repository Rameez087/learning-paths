10.4 Experiment tracking

"Which of the forty models I trained last month actually performed best, and what exact settings produced it?" — a question you should never have to answer from memory.

TL;DR
Experiment tracking tools (MLflow, Neptune, Weights & Biases) automatically log every training run's hyperparameters, metrics, and artifacts — so comparing model versions, reproducing a specific result, and auditing what actually happened are all queries against a structured record, not archaeology through scattered notebooks and half-remembered configurations. This connects directly to 5.5's reproducibility principle: given the logged configuration and data version, you should be able to reconstruct exactly how any past model was produced.

What gets logged, and why each piece matters

  • Hyperparameters — every setting used for that specific run, so a strong result can be traced back to exactly what produced it.
  • Metrics — accuracy, precision, recall, F1, ROC-AUC (10.3) computed automatically and logged per run, enabling direct side-by-side comparison across dozens of experiments.
  • Artifacts — the actual trained model file, confusion matrices, ROC curves, and other visualizations generated during evaluation, saved alongside the run's metadata.
  • Data version — which exact version of the training data was used, since 5.5's idempotency/reproducibility principle applies to ML pipelines too: the same code plus a different data snapshot can produce meaningfully different results.

Why this matters more as experimentation scales

Manually tracking a handful of experiments in a spreadsheet is tedious but survivable. Once a team is running dozens of hyperparameter combinations, comparing multiple model architectures, or iterating over weeks, manual tracking becomes both impractical and unreliable — details get lost, someone forgets which exact preprocessing steps a particular strong result actually used, and reproducing a result from three weeks ago becomes genuinely difficult. Automated tracking tools scale to this naturally because logging happens as a side effect of running the training code, not as a separate manual step someone has to remember to do.

Model registry: the step after tracking

Beyond tracking individual experiment runs, a model registry manages the lifecycle of models that get promoted toward production — versioning, stage transitions (staging, production, archived), and a clear audit trail of which specific tracked run became the model actually serving predictions. This is the connective tissue between the experimentation covered in this phase and the production concerns any deployed model eventually needs.

Check your understanding

1. What specific problem does experiment tracking solve as the number of training runs grows?
Manual tracking becomes unreliable at scale — automated tools solve this by making logging a side effect of running the code, not a separate step that can be forgotten or done inconsistently.
2. Why does logging the data version matter alongside hyperparameters?
This extends 5.5's reproducibility principle into ML experimentation specifically — code and configuration alone don't fully determine a result if the underlying data can also change between runs.
3. What does a model registry add on top of basic experiment tracking?
Experiment tracking covers the development/comparison phase; a model registry covers what happens after a model is chosen — managing its path toward and through production deployment.
