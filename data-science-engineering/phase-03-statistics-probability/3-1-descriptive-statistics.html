3.1 Descriptive statistics

One number can't summarize a dataset. Mean and median can disagree — and when they do, that disagreement is itself the insight.

TL;DR
Central tendency (mean, median, mode) answers "where's the middle?" — dispersion (variance, standard deviation, IQR) answers "how spread out is everything around that middle?" Reporting only one of these two is nearly always misleading: two datasets can share an identical mean while one is tightly clustered and the other wildly spread out. When mean and median genuinely disagree, that gap is diagnostic — it's telling you the distribution is skewed, and which measure you should actually trust depends on why.

Why mean vs. median tells a diagnostic story

Consider a dataset with mean $65,000 and median $45,000. The gap itself is the finding: it means the distribution is right-skewed — a long tail of a few very high earners is pulling the mean upward, while the median (unaffected by extreme values, since it only cares about position, not magnitude) reflects what a "typical" person in the dataset actually earns far more faithfully. This is exactly why household income is almost always reported as a median, not a mean.

Variance and standard deviation, precisely

Variance averages the squared distance of every point from the mean — squaring is what makes distances on either side of the mean add constructively instead of canceling out, but it also means variance is in squared units (dollars², not dollars), which is hard to interpret directly. Standard deviation is the square root of variance, bringing the units back to the original scale — this is why standard deviation, not variance, is almost always the number actually reported and interpreted.

For a normal distribution specifically, this gives a genuinely useful rule of thumb: roughly 68% of values fall within one standard deviation of the mean, about 95% within two, and about 99.7% within three — sometimes called the 68-95-99.7 rule.

Why IQR beats range when outliers are present

Range (max minus min) is the simplest spread measure, but it's maximally sensitive to a single extreme value — one data-entry error can make the range look enormous. The interquartile range (IQR = Q3 − Q1, the middle 50% of the data) is far more robust, because it deliberately ignores the most extreme quarter on each end. This is also the basis for a standard outlier-detection rule: a point more than 1.5×IQR beyond Q1 or Q3 is commonly flagged as a potential outlier.

Check your understanding

1. If a dataset's mean is much higher than its median, what does that most likely indicate?
Mean and median only coincide for perfectly symmetric distributions — a large gap between them is itself informative about skew, not an error.
2. Why is standard deviation reported far more often than variance?
Squaring distances to compute variance solves the cancellation problem but leaves the result in squared units — taking the square root (standard deviation) restores interpretability in the original scale.
3. Why does IQR handle outliers better than range?
Range is defined directly by the two most extreme points, so a single outlier controls it entirely — IQR's focus on the middle 50% makes it structurally robust to exactly that failure mode.
