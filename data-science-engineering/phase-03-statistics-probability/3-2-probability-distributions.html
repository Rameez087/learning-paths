3.2 Probability distributions

A distribution is a shape that tells you which outcomes are likely before you've even looked at the data.

TL;DR
A probability distribution describes how likely each possible value of a random variable is. The normal distribution (bell curve) is the most important one in practice — it emerges naturally whenever you're looking at a sum or average of many small independent effects, which is extremely common in real data. Its defining rule of thumb: about 68% of values fall within 1 standard deviation of the mean, about 95% within 2, and about 99.7% within 3 — the "68-95-99.7 rule."

Why the normal distribution shows up so often

The Central Limit Theorem is the deep reason: when you sum or average many independent random quantities — heights across a population, measurement errors, the average of samples drawn from almost any underlying distribution — the result tends toward a normal distribution regardless of what the original underlying distribution looked like. This is precisely why the normal distribution is the default assumption behind so much of statistics, including the hypothesis testing and confidence intervals covered next in 3.3 and 3.4.

Population vs. sample: a distinction that changes your formulas

Descriptive measures of an entire population use Greek letters (population mean μ, population standard deviation σ); descriptive measures computed from a sample use Roman letters (sample mean x̄, sample standard deviation s). This isn't just notation — sample statistics are estimates of unknown population parameters, and that estimation uncertainty is exactly what confidence intervals (3.4) are built to quantify.

Check your understanding

1. Roughly what percentage of values in a normal distribution fall within 2 standard deviations of the mean?
This is the middle figure in the 68-95-99.7 rule — roughly 95% of values fall within 2 standard deviations, with about 68% within 1 and about 99.7% within 3.
2. What does the Central Limit Theorem explain about why normal distributions appear so often in real data?
This is a statement about sums and averages converging toward normality, not a claim that every raw dataset is normal on its own — it's exactly why aggregated measurements so often look bell-shaped even when individual data points don't.
3. Why does it matter whether a statistic is a "population parameter" or a "sample statistic"?
This distinction is the entire foundation for confidence intervals and hypothesis testing (3.3-3.4) — the uncertainty in a sample-based estimate is precisely what those tools are built to quantify.
