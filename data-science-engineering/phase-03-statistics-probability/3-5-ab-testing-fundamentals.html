3.5 A/B testing fundamentals

Everything from this phase, applied to the single most common real-world use of statistics in tech.

TL;DR
An A/B test randomly splits users between a control and a variant, then uses hypothesis testing (3.3) to check whether an observed difference is real or just noise. The single most damaging, most common mistake in industry experimentation is the peeking problem: checking results early and stopping as soon as they look significant. Continuous monitoring of a fixed-horizon test can push the true false-positive rate from a nominal 5% past 30% — meaning roughly one in three "wins" declared this way isn't real. This isn't a fringe issue; it's described as the single most common statistical mistake in industry experimentation.

Why peeking inflates false positives so badly

Each daily check is, statistically, its own hypothesis test. Checking once and using α = 0.05 genuinely gives you a 5% false-positive rate. But checking 14 times and stopping the moment any single check looks significant means you've effectively run 14 chances to hit a false positive by pure noise — and noisy early data (small sample) is exactly when random swings are largest, making early "significant" results the least trustworthy ones, not the most exciting.

The fix: commit to a sample size before you start

A properly designed test calculates its required sample size before launch, based on your baseline conversion rate, the smallest effect size you actually care about detecting (the "minimum detectable effect"), and your desired statistical power. You then run the test until that sample size is reached and analyze once, at the predetermined endpoint — not whenever the dashboard happens to look good. If checking in in the meantime is operationally necessary, use a designed sequential testing method (alpha spending), which mathematically adjusts the significance threshold at each check specifically to keep the overall false-positive rate at the nominal level despite multiple looks.

A common misconception: "I'll just switch to Bayesian testing"

Bayesian A/B testing does solve a real problem — communicating "there's a 94% probability Variant B is better" is far more intuitive to a business stakeholder than "we reject the null hypothesis at α = 0.05." But it is specifically flagged as a misconception that this switch alone fixes the peeking problem — continuously monitoring a Bayesian test and stopping when the posterior looks favorable carries its own, related risk of premature stopping on noise. The fix for peeking is a properly designed stopping rule, not a change of statistical framework alone.

Sample ratio mismatch: the sanity check nobody skips

Before trusting any A/B test result, check that users were actually split into the expected proportions (e.g. roughly 50/50). A meaningful deviation — a sample ratio mismatch — usually signals a bug in the randomization or logging pipeline, and any result from a test with this problem should be treated as untrustworthy regardless of how significant it looks.

Check your understanding

1. Why does checking A/B test results daily and stopping at the first significant result inflate the false-positive rate?
This is the core mechanism of the peeking problem — the nominal 5% false-positive guarantee assumes a single look at the data; multiple looks without correction compound the risk far beyond that nominal rate.
2. Does switching to Bayesian A/B testing solve the peeking problem?
This is explicitly flagged as a popular misconception — Bayesian framing improves how results are communicated, but doesn't on its own fix the statistical risk of stopping early based on favorable-looking interim results.
3. What does a sample ratio mismatch usually indicate?
If users weren't split into the intended proportions, something upstream of the statistics is broken — this is a data-integrity check that should be verified before trusting any result from the test, however clean the p-value looks.
