3.3 Hypothesis testing

A structured way to ask "is this difference real, or could it just be random noise?"

TL;DR
Hypothesis testing starts by assuming the null hypothesis (H₀) — usually "no effect, no difference" — is true, then asks how surprising the observed data would be under that assumption. The p-value is that surprise, quantified: the probability of seeing a result at least this extreme if H₀ really were true. A small p-value (conventionally below a significance level α, often 0.05) means the data would be quite surprising under H₀, leading you to reject it. Critically: a p-value does NOT tell you the probability that H₀ is true — that's one of the most common misinterpretations in the field.

What the p-value actually measures — and doesn't

A p-value of 0.03 means: "if there truly were no effect, there'd be a 3% chance of seeing a result this extreme or more, purely from random sampling variation." It does not mean "there's a 3% chance the null hypothesis is true," and it does not measure how large or practically important an effect is — a tiny, meaningless effect can still produce a very small p-value if the sample size is large enough. This is exactly why statistical significance and practical significance are different questions, and confusing the two is flagged as a common, consequential mistake even among experienced researchers.

The trade-off between the two error types

Lowering your significance level α (say, from 0.05 to 0.01) makes Type I errors less likely — but for the same sample size, it makes Type II errors more likely, because you're demanding stronger evidence before rejecting H₀. Power (1 − β) is the probability of correctly detecting a real effect when one exists — an underpowered study (too small a sample) may simply lack the ability to detect an effect even if it's genuinely there, which is a different failure than "there's no effect to find."

One-tailed vs. two-tailed tests

A two-tailed test checks for a difference in either direction ("is the new version different from the old, better or worse?"). A one-tailed test checks for a difference in only one specific direction ("is the new version better?"). A one-tailed test has more power to detect an effect in the direction you specified — but committing to a direction ahead of time is a real methodological choice, not something to pick after seeing which direction the data happened to go.

Check your understanding

1. What does a p-value of 0.03 actually mean?
This is the single most common misinterpretation to avoid — a p-value is a statement about how surprising the data is under H₀, not a probability statement about H₀ itself.
2. What happens to Type II error risk when you lower your significance level α (e.g. from 0.05 to 0.01), holding sample size fixed?
The two error types trade off against each other for a fixed sample size — making it harder to reject H₀ (lower α) reduces false positives but increases the chance of missing a real effect (false negatives).
3. Why is choosing a one-tailed test after seeing which direction your data went considered problematic?
Picking the direction retroactively after seeing the data gives you an unfair advantage in finding "significance" — the test's validity depends on the direction being specified as part of the original hypothesis, before the data is examined.
