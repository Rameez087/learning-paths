9.5 Regularization

A deliberate penalty for complexity — sometimes the best way to make a model perform better is to stop it from trying so hard.

TL;DR
Regularization adds a penalty term to a model's loss function based on the size of its coefficients, directly countering the high-variance/overfitting side of 9.4's tradeoff. The two dominant forms: L2 (Ridge) penalizes the sum of squared coefficients, shrinking all of them toward zero without fully eliminating any; L1 (Lasso) penalizes the sum of absolute coefficient values, which can shrink some coefficients all the way to exactly zero — effectively performing feature selection (9.3) as a side effect of regularization.

Why more regularization means "more balanced, not perfect fit"

Regularization deliberately introduces some bias in exchange for reduced variance — a regularized model typically won't fit the training data as precisely as an unregularized one would, but it tends to perform better on new, unseen data. This is 9.4's tradeoff, made directly controllable via a single tunable parameter (commonly called λ or alpha) rather than something you only influence indirectly through model architecture choices.

Choosing between L1 and L2 (or both)

  • Use L1 (Lasso) when you suspect many features are irrelevant and want automatic feature selection built into training — the zeroed-out coefficients directly tell you which features the model decided not to use.
  • Use L2 (Ridge) when you believe most features carry at least some signal and want to shrink their influence without eliminating any entirely — often more stable when features are correlated with each other.
  • Elastic Net combines both penalties, useful when you want some feature selection along with Ridge's stability, particularly with correlated features.

Regularization isn't the only tool — and often isn't even the first one

Recall from 9.4: more data is described as the most effective regularizer of all — genuinely more diverse training examples often reduce overfitting more reliably than any penalty term. Regularization, cross-validation, feature selection, and gathering more data all attack the same underlying variance problem from different angles, and production practice usually combines several of them rather than relying on just one.

Check your understanding

1. What's the key practical difference between L1 and L2 regularization?
This is the defining practical distinction — L1's absolute-value penalty geometry allows exact zeros, while L2's squared penalty geometry only shrinks coefficients asymptotically toward (but not to) zero.
2. Why does increasing regularization strength typically increase a model's training-data bias?
This is the bias-variance tradeoff (9.4) made directly tunable — regularization intentionally accepts some bias increase in exchange for a variance decrease, when that trade improves overall generalization.
3. According to the material, what's described as the most effective regularizer of all?
This reinforces 9.4's point directly — while L1/L2 penalties are useful and tunable, more genuine training data often reduces overfitting more fundamentally than any penalty term can.
