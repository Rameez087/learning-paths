9.4 Bias-variance tradeoff

Not abstract theory — a diagnostic lens senior candidates are specifically expected to apply to every training run.

TL;DR
Bias is error from a model being too simple — it misses real signal in the data (underfitting). Variance is error from a model being too reactive — it memorizes noise and quirks specific to the training data rather than learning the true underlying pattern (overfitting). As model complexity increases, bias tends to decrease while variance increases — this produces a U-shaped total error curve, and the goal is finding the complexity level near the bottom of that U, not the extreme of either end.

How to actually diagnose which problem you have

A practical diagnostic senior candidates are specifically expected to articulate: use learning curves and a clean validation strategy (10.1's train/validation/test split). If both training and validation error are high, that's high bias — the model is too rigid, missing real signal. If training error is low but validation error is much higher, that's high variance — the model has memorized training-specific quirks that don't generalize.

What actually fixes each problem

  • High bias fixes — increase model complexity, add more informative features, use a less constrained model family, or relax regularization (9.5).
  • High variance fixes — simplify or stabilize the model, apply stronger regularization, remove leaky or high-cardinality features, or gather more training data if genuinely available (more data is described as the most effective regularizer of all).

A specifically named trap to avoid: don't try to "tune your way out" of what's actually a data problem. If the training data itself is too small, too noisy, or missing genuinely predictive features, no amount of hyperparameter adjustment on the model side will fully compensate.

Cross-validation: getting a more reliable read on where you stand

A single train/validation split can give a misleading picture purely from how the split happened to fall. Cross-validation divides the data into multiple folds, trains and tests across different combinations of those folds, and averages the results — reducing the risk that a model looking good is really just a model that got lucky with one particular split. Check your understanding

1. What does it mean for a model to have high bias?
Bias is specifically about a model being too rigid or constrained to represent the true pattern — the opposite failure mode from variance, which is about being too flexible.
2. How would you diagnose high variance using training and validation error?
This gap — good on training data, poor on unseen validation data — is the signature of overfitting, distinct from high bias where both errors are high because the model can't fit even the training data well.
3. Why is it a mistake to try to "tune your way out" of what's actually a data problem?
This is a specifically named practitioner caution — model-side adjustments have real limits, and correctly diagnosing a data-quality root cause (rather than repeatedly re-tuning) is what actually solves the problem.
