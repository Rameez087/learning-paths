9.1 Supervised vs. unsupervised learning

The question that decides everything else: does your data come with the "right answers" already attached?

TL;DR
In supervised learning, the training data includes labels — the correct answer for each example — and the model learns to map inputs to those known outputs (predict house price from features, classify an email as spam or not). In unsupervised learning, no labels exist; the model finds structure in the data on its own — grouping similar customers (clustering), reducing many features to a few meaningful ones (dimensionality reduction), or flagging unusual data points (anomaly detection). Unsupervised learning shows up constantly in production — recommendation engines, fraud detection, log analysis — and is genuinely worth taking as seriously as supervised learning, not treating as a footnote.

Why the distinction changes everything downstream

Supervised learning gives you a clear, measurable target — you can directly check "did the model predict close to the correct answer?" against held-out labeled data (Phase 10 covers this rigorously). Unsupervised learning has no such ground truth to check against — evaluating whether a clustering result is "good" is a genuinely harder, more subjective problem, often requiring domain expertise to judge whether the discovered groups actually make business sense rather than a clean numeric accuracy score.

Where each type shows up in real production systems

  • Supervised — classification: fraud/not-fraud, spam/not-spam, churn/no-churn.
  • Supervised — regression: predicting a continuous number — house price, demand forecast, delivery time.
  • Unsupervised — clustering: customer segmentation, grouping similar support tickets.
  • Unsupervised — dimensionality reduction: compressing hundreds of correlated features into a handful that capture most of the signal, often as a preprocessing step before a supervised model.
  • Unsupervised — anomaly detection: flagging a transaction, log entry, or sensor reading that looks nothing like the rest of the data.

A third category worth naming: reinforcement learning

Reinforcement learning is the third major paradigm — an agent learns by taking actions in an environment and receiving rewards or penalties, gradually learning a policy that maximizes cumulative reward. It's less commonly the first tool reached for in typical business data science work, but it's the foundation behind game-playing AI and increasingly some recommendation and resource-allocation systems.

Check your understanding

1. What's the defining difference between supervised and unsupervised learning?
The presence or absence of labeled "correct answers" in the training data is the fundamental distinction driving everything else — the type of model, the training objective, and how you'd evaluate results.
2. Why is evaluating an unsupervised model's output generally harder than evaluating a supervised one?
Supervised learning's labels provide a direct, objective comparison target; unsupervised learning's lack of labels means "is this clustering good?" is a much more subjective, context-dependent question.
3. What distinguishes reinforcement learning from both supervised and unsupervised learning?
Reinforcement learning's interactive, reward-driven structure is genuinely distinct from learning a fixed mapping (supervised) or discovering structure (unsupervised) in a static dataset.
