9.2 Classical ML algorithms

Before reaching for a neural network, these algorithms solve most real business problems — faster to train, easier to explain, often just as accurate on tabular data.

TL;DR
Current 2026 guidance for learning machine learning is explicit about sequencing: start with supervised learning — regression, classification, decision trees, ensemble methods — using plain tools (scikit-learn, NumPy, pandas). None of the fundamentals require a GPU. These "classical" algorithms remain the right default for most tabular business data, and understanding them deeply (including working through gradient descent by hand at least once) is what makes the rest of the field legible, including deep learning later.

Regression vs. classification: same algorithms, different targets

Linear regression predicts a continuous number (a price, a duration). Logistic regression, despite the name, is actually for classification — it predicts a probability between 0 and 1 (email is spam) by applying a sigmoid function to what would otherwise be a linear regression's output. Understanding this connection is genuinely useful: the two aren't unrelated algorithms that happen to share a name, one is a direct extension of the other for a different type of target variable.

Why gradient descent is worth doing by hand once

Nobody manually computes gradient descent on the job — libraries handle this. The value of working through the math by hand at least once is that it makes the rest of the field legible: hyperparameters like learning rate, the reason training can diverge or get stuck, and why deep learning training dynamics behave the way they do all trace back to this same underlying optimization process, just at much larger scale.

Ensemble methods: why combining weak models often beats one strong one

Ensemble methods (Random Forests, Gradient Boosting, XGBoost) combine predictions from multiple individual models to produce more stable, accurate results than any single model alone. This works because different models tend to make different errors — averaging or combining them cancels out some of each individual model's mistakes. XGBoost specifically integrates with distributed systems like Spark (8.2) and Flink, with built-in cross-validation at each boosting iteration.

Check your understanding

1. Despite its name, what does logistic regression actually do?
Logistic regression is a classification algorithm built as a direct extension of linear regression — the name reflects its mathematical lineage, not its actual output type.
2. Why do ensemble methods often outperform a single model?
The core insight behind ensembling is diversity of errors — models that fail in different ways, when combined, tend to be more robust than any one model alone.
3. Why do current 2026 learning roadmaps recommend starting with classical algorithms before deep learning?
This is a pedagogical and practical sequencing choice, not a claim of universal superiority — classical methods build foundational understanding and remain genuinely useful for the tabular data that dominates most business use cases.
