11.1 Data contracts

"Data quality is not a tool — it's a practice that starts with an agreement between whoever produces the data and whoever depends on it."

TL;DR
A data contract is a formal agreement between a data producer and its consumers, defining schema, semantics, freshness SLAs, and governance rules — turning what used to be an implicit, easily-broken assumption ("that column will always be there") into something explicit, testable, and enforced. The critical design principle: consumers should never be the first to discover a contract violation — validation runs on the producer side, and the producer is notified before or at the moment data would be delivered broken, not after a downstream dashboard has already shown wrong numbers to a business user.

What a data contract actually specifies

  • Schema — exact column names, types, and whether a column is required or nullable.
  • Semantics — what a column actually means (is "revenue" gross or net? in which currency?), preventing the same field name from silently meaning different things to different teams.
  • SLAs — freshness guarantees (data will be updated by 6am daily) and other timing commitments.
  • Governance rules — access controls, PII handling requirements, retention policies tied to the specific dataset.

Enforcement: contracts are only as good as their automation

A contract that exists only as a document nobody checks against provides essentially no real protection. In practice, contract checks (schema validation, freshness tests, quality rules) get built into the pipeline itself using tools like Great Expectations or Soda, running as validation steps integrated directly into the orchestrator (Airflow, Prefect, Dagster — Phase 7). Modern warehouses' rich information schemas can often be queried directly with plain SQL to validate governance rules as well.

Discoverability: a contract nobody can find isn't useful

Every contract should be published to a data catalog so a consumer evaluating whether to use a dataset can see exactly what the producer has committed to — freshness, schema, semantics — before building something that depends on it. A contract that exists but isn't discoverable provides little more protection than no contract at all, since consumers have no way to know what guarantees actually exist.

Check your understanding

1. What is the key design principle behind where data contract validation should run?
Catching a violation before delivery (producer-side) prevents broken data from ever reaching a downstream dashboard or model — this is specifically what separates a genuinely protective contract from a documentation exercise.
2. Why does a data contract's "semantics" specification matter beyond just schema (column names and types)?
A schema check alone (column exists, correct type) can't catch a meaning mismatch — semantics documentation specifically closes this gap by making the intended interpretation explicit.
3. Why does a data contract need to be published to a catalog rather than just existing as an internal document?
Discoverability and enforcement are separate concerns — a catalog entry makes the contract's terms visible to consumers, which is necessary but not sufficient (automated validation is what actually enforces it).
