7.4 Orchestration observability

Knowing a pipeline is running is easy. Knowing whether it's healthy takes a specific set of metrics, watched continuously.

TL;DR
Orchestration observability means tracking task duration, DAG run frequency, scheduler lag, and worker availability — typically via Prometheus/Grafana or a dedicated platform like Datadog — layered on top of the orchestrator's own built-in logging and alerting. This is specifically about the health of the orchestration layer itself: is the scheduler keeping up, are tasks taking longer than usual, are workers available — a different concern from data quality observability (Phase 11), which asks whether the data flowing through is actually correct.

Scheduler lag: the metric most teams don't watch until it's a crisis

Scheduler lag measures the gap between when a task was supposed to start and when it actually started. A growing lag usually means the scheduler itself is overloaded — too many DAGs, too much parsing overhead, insufficient scheduler resources — and it's a leading indicator: tasks might still all be technically succeeding while quietly starting later and later, which eventually cascades into missed SLAs (7.3) across the entire platform, not just one pipeline.

Alert routing: getting the right signal to the right channel

Built-in orchestrator alerting typically supports routing to email, Slack, PagerDuty, or a metrics platform. The practical design question isn't just "does an alert fire" but "does it reach the person who can actually act on it, with enough context to act quickly" — a scheduler-lag alert buried in the same channel as routine daily success notifications is functionally the same as no alert at all, since it gets lost in noise.

Orchestration observability vs. data observability: keep them separate

This is worth stating precisely, echoing 7.2's "green DAG run" point: orchestration observability answers "is the pipeline machinery healthy" (is the scheduler keeping up, are tasks finishing on time, are workers available). Data observability (Phase 11) answers "is the data itself healthy" (is it fresh, complete, matching expected patterns). A platform can have perfect orchestration health — every task green, no lag, no SLA misses — while still silently processing corrupted or incomplete data. Both layers are necessary; neither substitutes for the other.

Check your understanding

1. What does a growing scheduler lag typically indicate?
Scheduler lag is specifically an orchestration-layer health signal, distinct from a task-level failure or a data quality issue — it's about the scheduling infrastructure itself falling behind.
2. Why is alert routing described as being about more than just "does an alert fire"?
The practical value of an alert depends on it reaching someone who can act on it with enough urgency and context — buried or noisy alerting undermines the entire point of having alerts at all.
3. Why can't perfect orchestration observability substitute for data observability?
This is the direct extension of 7.2's "green DAG run" limitation — orchestration health and data health are independent properties, and a platform needs both types of observability to be genuinely trustworthy.
