5.3 Chunking strategies

Where you cut a document decides what a query can and can't find.

TL;DR
A document has to be split into smaller pieces before embedding — you can't usefully embed an entire book as one vector. Chunks that are too small lose surrounding context; chunks too large dilute the specific point a query is looking for inside irrelevant surrounding text. Overlap between consecutive chunks prevents a fact from being awkwardly split across a chunk boundary. There's no universal "correct" chunk size — it's an empirical parameter tuned against real retrieval quality on your own documents and queries.

Fixed-size vs. semantic chunking

  • Fixed-size (with overlap) — split every N characters or tokens, with a small overlapping window between consecutive chunks (e.g. 500 tokens per chunk, 50 tokens of overlap). Simple, deterministic, and the same document always splits the same way — useful when you need reproducibility as you iterate on other parts of the pipeline.
  • Semantic chunking — split at natural boundaries (paragraph breaks, section headings, sentence groups that discuss one coherent idea) rather than a fixed character count. This tends to keep a complete thought inside one chunk rather than slicing it arbitrarily in the middle, at the cost of variable chunk sizes and more preprocessing complexity.

Always carry metadata alongside the chunk

Production RAG systems store more than raw chunk text — source document, section heading, page number, and a parent-chunk ID are standard alongside the embedding itself. This metadata enables citation (showing the user exactly where an answer came from), filtering (searching only within a specific document set), and hierarchical retrieval (finding a small precise chunk, then pulling in its parent section for broader context if needed).

Why "naive" chunking fails so often in practice

Industry reporting on production RAG deployments cites naive pipelines failing at retrieval a substantial fraction of the time — a large share of that failure traces back to chunking decisions made without testing: chunks too small to carry meaning on their own, chunks that split a fact and its qualifying context across a boundary, or no overlap at all so a sentence that spans two chunks becomes unretrievable in its complete form by either.

Check your understanding

1. What problem does overlap between consecutive chunks solve?
Without overlap, a sentence spanning a chunk boundary can end up split between two chunks, with neither one containing the complete thought — overlap gives that content a chance to appear intact in at least one chunk.
2. What is the main trade-off of semantic chunking versus fixed-size chunking?
Semantic chunking respects natural content boundaries rather than a fixed character count, which better preserves coherent ideas but requires more sophisticated splitting logic and produces less predictable chunk sizes.
3. Why store metadata (source, section, page, parent ID) alongside each chunk's embedding?
The embedding alone only supports similarity search — metadata is what lets a production system show the user where an answer came from, restrict search scope, and pull in surrounding context when a chunk alone isn't enough.
