1.1 Meet Chirp: why starting simple is correct

You're the only engineer at a two-person startup. Chirp — short posts, a feed, follows — just went live. It runs on one server. That's not a mistake you'll fix later. It's the correct decision, made for the right reasons, that you'll need to unmake later for equally right reasons.

"We shipped Chirp last night. 40 signups so far, mostly friends. It's just... running. One DigitalOcean droplet, Postgres and the app on the same box. Feels almost too simple to call 'architecture.'" — you, in the team Slack, launch day
TL;DR
This whole track is one continuous story: Chirp grows from 500 users to 100 million, and at nearly every stage, something that worked perfectly well at the previous scale breaks — predictably, for a specific, explainable reason. You'll meet the fix, then meet the new problem the fix quietly introduces, which becomes the next chapter's bottleneck. Nothing here is taught before it's needed. That's deliberate: system design interviews test whether you can reason from constraints to a defensible design, not whether you've memorized a list of technologies — and reasoning is exactly what this format forces you to practice.

Why the "overbuilt" version is actually the wrong answer

It's tempting to think the disciplined move is designing for scale from day one — sharded database, message queues, a Kubernetes cluster, multi-region from the start. It isn't. At 500 users, none of that infrastructure has a job to do yet, and every piece of it is something you now have to operate, monitor, and pay for while getting zero benefit in return. Interviewers specifically penalize this instinct at the junior level — the signal it sends isn't "prepared," it's "doesn't understand what problem each piece of infrastructure actually solves."

The one thing worth doing on day one anyway

Simple doesn't mean careless. Even at 500 users, Chirp's code separates cleanly into layers (routes, business logic, data access) and the database schema is sensibly normalized — not because the current scale demands it, but because clean separation costs almost nothing to maintain now and makes every future change (including every fix in this track) meaningfully easier to make correctly. The distinction that matters: architectural complexity you don't need yet is expensive and premature; code hygiene you'll always want is nearly free.

How to read this track

Every chapter from here follows a consistent shape: a Bottleneck page showing exactly what's breaking and why, with real (fictional but realistic) diagnostic data — a graph, a spiking metric, an alert. Then a Fix page explaining the solution and why it works. Most chapters end with a New Problem page — the side effect the fix quietly introduces, which becomes the next chapter's bottleneck. After the ten growth chapters, Phase 11 consolidates the vocabulary into reference form, and Phases 12-13 apply everything to the classic named interview problems.

Check your understanding

1. Why is designing Chirp for massive scale on day one considered a mistake, not just unnecessary caution?
This is specifically why interviewers penalize premature complexity — it signals a mismatch between the proposed architecture and the actual stated requirements, not technical sophistication.
2. What's the distinction this page draws between "architectural complexity you don't need yet" and "code hygiene you'll always want"?
This is the actual nuance behind "start simple" — it's not an argument for sloppiness, just for not building infrastructure that has no current job to do.
3. Why does this track deliberately teach every concept only at the point Chirp's story actually needs it?
This is the core pedagogical bet of the whole track — motivation-first learning builds the same reasoning muscle the actual interview tests, rather than just recognition of terms.
