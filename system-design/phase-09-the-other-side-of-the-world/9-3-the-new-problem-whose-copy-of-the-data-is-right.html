New Problem

9.3 The new problem: whose copy of the data is right?

A user in Singapore updates their bio. Their friend in Virginia, viewing their profile a second later, sees the old one. Both regions are healthy. Neither is technically wrong.

"This is basically Chapter 5's replication lag problem again, except now the 'replica' is an entire region on the other side of the planet, and the lag isn't milliseconds — it can be a genuinely noticeable fraction of a second, sometimes more under real network conditions." — you, recognizing the pattern immediately
TL;DR
9.2 deliberately left open whether regions share one database or each get their own — and either answer creates a real consistency challenge. If each region has its own writable database (needed for fast local writes), the same profile update made in Singapore has to eventually reach Virginia's copy — and until it does, the two regions genuinely disagree about the current truth. This is Chapter 5.3's replication lag problem, reappearing at a much larger scale: not milliseconds between two machines in the same data center, but a real, sometimes substantial delay between machines on opposite sides of the planet.

Try it: watch a cross-region update propagate

Why this can't be fixed the same simple way as 5.3

5.3's fix — route a user's own recent reads back to the primary — worked because there was one clear primary to route back to. If both Virginia and Singapore accept writes locally (the whole reason multi-region deployment helps write latency, not just reads), there's no single "the real one" to fall back to anymore. Both regions' databases are simultaneously legitimate sources of truth for their own local writes — this is a genuinely harder problem than 5.3's, not just a bigger version of it.

Three real approaches, each with a real trade-off

  • Single-region writes, multi-region reads — only Virginia accepts writes; Singapore serves fast local reads but forwards writes back to Virginia. Simple and consistent, but Singapore users get slow writes (defeating part of the original point).
  • Multi-region writes with conflict resolution — both regions accept local writes, and a defined rule (like "most recent timestamp wins") resolves disagreements when they're detected. Fast writes everywhere, but genuinely possible to lose an update if two conflicting writes happen close together.
  • Accept eventual consistency explicitly — let both regions temporarily disagree, guarantee they'll converge given enough time, and design the product to tolerate brief disagreement gracefully (much like 4.4 and 5.3's targeted, narrow fixes rather than eliminating the underlying lag entirely).

Why Chirp chooses the third option, for now

For most of Chirp's data — bios, posts, follower counts — being briefly, slightly stale across regions is an acceptable trade-off, the same reasoning from 4.2 and 5.2 applied at a larger scale. The genuinely hard cases (payment processing, account security settings) would need the first, more conservative approach — a reminder that "the right consistency model" isn't one global answer, it's a decision made per type of data, based on how much a brief disagreement actually costs.
