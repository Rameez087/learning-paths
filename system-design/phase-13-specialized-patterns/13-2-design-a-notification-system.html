13.2 Design a notification system

Chirp's Notifications service (Chapter 7 and 8) as a standalone, general-purpose interview problem — plus the parts the narrative simplified past.

TL;DR
Requirements: send notifications across multiple channels (push, email, SMS) triggered by many different events, respecting each user's preferences, without overwhelming anyone. Architecturally this is Chapter 7.2's message queue and workers pattern directly — but a general-purpose notification system needs one more layer: a preference and throttling check before fan-out, since a user might want push notifications for DMs but not for every like, and definitely doesn't want 50 separate notifications in five minutes.

Try it: route a notification through preference and throttle checks

Why preference checking has to happen before fan-out, not after

Checking preferences after generating a notification wastes the fan-out work Chapter 7.2 was specifically designed to make efficient. The correct order: check the user's channel preferences and current throttle state first (a fast key-value lookup, 11.4), and only publish to the queue for channels the user actually wants and hasn't been over-notified through — the same "cheap gatekeeper before expensive work" instinct as 11.5's bloom filter pattern, applied to a different kind of check.

The deep-dive: batching and digesting, not just sending or not sending

Beyond a binary on/off preference, real notification systems batch related notifications — "5 people liked your post" instead of 5 separate pushes — computed by a short delay window before sending (accumulate events for a few minutes, then send one combined notification). This adds a small amount of latency in exchange for a dramatically better user experience, a genuinely different trade-off shape than most of this track's latency-vs-consistency choices.

Delivery across channels: not all channels are equally reliable

Push notifications can silently fail (an uninstalled app, an expired device token) with no error the sending system necessarily sees immediately. Email and SMS have real per-message costs and stricter rate limits from third-party providers. A robust design tracks delivery status per channel and can retry-with-idempotency (7.3's pattern again) or fall back to a different channel if the preferred one seems to be failing silently.
