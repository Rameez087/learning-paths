13.4 Design a payment system

Chirp sells premium subscriptions now. This is the one domain in this entire track where "eventually consistent" is genuinely not an acceptable answer.

TL;DR
Requirements: process a payment exactly once, never double-charge, never lose a transaction, even under network failures and retries. This is Chapter 7.3's idempotency lesson at its highest possible stakes — every payment request carries an idempotency key, and the system guarantees that retrying the same key never results in a second charge. Unlike nearly every other design in this track, a payment system deliberately chooses consistency over availability (11.3's CAP framework) at almost every decision point — better to briefly reject a request than risk an incorrect charge.

Try it: watch idempotent payment handling prevent a double charge

Why this domain flips nearly every trade-off made throughout the track

Chirp's feed happily served slightly stale data (4.2), replicas happily lagged behind their primary (5.2), regions happily disagreed briefly (9.3) — all deliberate, reasonable choices for a social app. A payment can't "eventually" be correct — a user needs to know definitively whether they were charged, and the system needs to guarantee it happened exactly once. This isn't a criticism of any earlier chapter's choices; it's the same CAP/PACELC framework (11.3) producing a genuinely different answer because the actual cost of an error is categorically different here.

The deep-dive: the saga pattern, applied to money instead of account deletion

A purchase often touches multiple systems: charge the payment provider, grant the subscription entitlement, send a receipt — genuinely the same multi-step saga shape from Chapter 8.3, just with a much higher cost of getting the compensating action wrong. If granting the entitlement fails after the charge succeeds, the compensating action (refunding the charge) has to be exactly as reliable as the original charge — this is precisely why payment sagas are considered one of the hardest real-world instances of the pattern.

Working with a payment processor: someone else's system of record

Unlike every other design in this track, Chirp doesn't own the actual money-movement infrastructure — a third-party payment processor (Stripe-like) does. This means the idempotency key has to be honored not just by Chirp's own services, but by the processor's API too, extending 11.1's idempotent API design principle across an organizational boundary, not just an internal one.

Check your understanding

1. Why does a payment system generally choose consistency over availability, unlike most of Chirp's other design decisions throughout this track?
This is the same framework (11.3) producing a different, equally valid answer — the right trade-off always depends on what a specific error actually costs, not a universal rule.
2. Why is a payment saga's compensating action (refunding a charge) considered especially high-stakes compared to Chapter 8.3's account-deletion saga?
This is the same pattern (8.3's saga) reapplied to a domain where the cost of getting the compensation wrong is dramatically higher — the mechanism is identical, the stakes are not.
3. Why does idempotency need to extend to the third-party payment processor's API, not just Chirp's own internal services?
This is a genuine, practical extension of the idempotency principle into a real-world constraint — depending on external systems means the guarantee has to hold across that boundary too.
