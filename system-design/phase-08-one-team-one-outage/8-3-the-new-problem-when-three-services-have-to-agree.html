New Problem

8.3 The new problem: when three services have to agree

Deleting an account used to be one database transaction. Now it's three separate services, each doing their own part — and one of them just failed halfway through.

"User requested account deletion. Profile Service deleted the account. Feed Service deleted their posts. Search Service... timed out. Now there's a user with no profile and no posts, but their old content is still fully searchable. That's a real, visible inconsistency, and there's no single database transaction that could have prevented it anymore." — you, filing a bug
TL;DR
Before microservices, an operation touching multiple pieces of data could be one atomic database transaction — either everything succeeds together, or nothing does, guaranteed by the database itself. Once that data is owned by separate services with separate databases (8.2), there's no single transaction spanning all of them anymore — each service's part either succeeds or fails independently, and a failure partway through leaves the overall operation in a genuinely inconsistent state. The saga pattern is the standard answer: instead of one atomic transaction, a sequence of local transactions, each with a defined compensating action that undoes it if a later step fails.

Try it: walk through a saga handling a failure

Compensating actions: undoing, not just retrying

When Search Service's deletion step fails, the saga doesn't simply retry forever — it triggers compensating actions for every step that already succeeded: Feed Service restores the deleted posts, Profile Service restores the account. The system deliberately moves back toward a consistent state rather than being left in an ambiguous, half-finished one. This is meaningfully different from Chapter 7.3's idempotency fix — that was about safely handling duplicate delivery of the same action; this is about safely undoing a partially-completed sequence of different actions across different services.

Why this is a genuinely harder problem than anything earlier in the track

Every fix through Chapter 7 kept a single source of truth for any given piece of data, even when that data was replicated (Chapter 5) or sharded (Chapter 6) — there was always one authoritative place. Microservices deliberately give up a single, unified transaction boundary in exchange for independent deployability (8.2's real benefit) — and the saga pattern is the honest, practical cost of that trade-off: coordinating consistency across services takes real, deliberate design work that a single database used to provide for free.

The two saga styles, briefly

A choreographed saga has each service listen for events and decide its own next action independently — no central coordinator, but harder to reason about as steps grow. An orchestrated saga has one central coordinator explicitly calling each service in sequence and triggering compensations on failure — easier to reason about and debug, at the cost of that coordinator becoming an important, carefully-designed piece of infrastructure in its own right.
