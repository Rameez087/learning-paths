8.2 The fix: breaking Chirp into services

Feed, Notifications, Search, and Profile become four separate applications — deployed independently, failing independently, owned by four separate teams.

"Split the monolith into services this quarter — Feed, Notifications, Search, Profile, each with its own deploy pipeline and its own on-call rotation. Yesterday, notifications had a bad deploy and rolled back on their own, in about 90 seconds, without anyone on the feed team even noticing." — you, three months later
TL;DR
Microservices split one large application into several smaller, independently deployable services, each owning a specific piece of functionality and its own data. Services communicate over the network (typically via APIs) rather than through in-process function calls, which means a crash in one service no longer automatically crashes another — directly solving 8.1's coupling problem. Each team can now deploy their own service on their own schedule, without coordinating with every other team touching the same shared codebase.

Drawing service boundaries around data, not just code

The most important design decision in this split isn't which files go where — it's which service owns which data. The Notifications service owns the notifications table entirely; other services never query it directly, only through Notifications' own API. This matters because it's what actually enables independent deployment: if the Feed service could directly query the Notifications database, changing that database's schema would still require coordinating with Feed's team, undoing much of the point of splitting them apart.

Why this doesn't come for free — the honest cost

What used to be a single, fast, in-process function call between two features is now a real network request between two separate services — slower, and now genuinely capable of failing in ways an in-process call never could (the other service being down, a network timeout, partial data returned). Every one of this track's earlier network-related lessons — Chapter 2's app/DB split, Chapter 3's load balancing, Chapter 5's replication — now applies again, but between many more independent pieces instead of just two.

Why 40 engineers specifically makes this trade-off worth it

At small team sizes, a monolith's simplicity (one codebase, one deploy, no network calls between features) genuinely outweighs microservices' operational complexity — this is exactly why Chirp correctly stayed a monolith through Chapters 1-7. The trade-off flips specifically once team coordination overhead (8.1) becomes a bigger cost than the added network complexity microservices introduce. There's no universal "right" architecture here — only the one that matches the actual current constraint, the same principle from Chapter 1.1, now playing out at organizational scale.

Check your understanding

1. Why does splitting Chirp into separate services directly solve 8.1's crash-coupling problem?
This is the direct mechanism — process isolation between services is what prevents one feature's failure from taking down unrelated features, unlike the shared-process monolith.
2. Why does drawing service boundaries around data ownership matter more than just splitting code into separate files?
This is the deeper design principle — genuine independence requires each service to own its data exclusively, not just having separate code repositories.
3. Why was staying a monolith the correct choice for Chirp through Chapters 1-7, given that microservices are being introduced now?
This reinforces Chapter 1.1's foundational theme one more time — the right architecture matches the actual current constraint, and that constraint genuinely changed as the team grew.
