12.2 Design a social feed

"The single most important system design concept," according to multiple sources — and you've been building toward it since Chapter 4. Now it gets named directly: fan-out.

TL;DR
Requirements: show each user a feed of recent posts from people they follow, fast. The central design question is fan-out — when should the work of "who needs to see this post" happen? Fan-out on write (push): the instant a post is created, immediately write it into every follower's feed — feed reads become instant, but posting gets expensive for popular accounts (exactly Chapter 6.4's celebrity problem). Fan-out on read (pull): compute a user's feed at request time by fetching recent posts from everyone they follow — posting stays cheap, but reads get expensive, especially for users who follow many accounts (Chapter 4.1's original bottleneck).

Try it: compare fan-out strategies for different account sizes

The hybrid approach: what Chirp actually needs, honestly

Neither pure strategy works well at both extremes. The practical answer, and what real social platforms actually do: push for most accounts (fast reads for the common case), pull for celebrity accounts above some follower threshold — exactly Chapter 6.4's "special-case the extremes" pattern, applied directly to fan-out design. A user's feed becomes a merge: pre-computed pushed posts from normal accounts, plus a live pull for any celebrities they follow, combined at read time.

The deep-dive: where the pushed feed actually lives

A pushed feed is naturally a key-value structure (11.4) — "user 4821's feed" mapping to a list of recent post IDs, exactly the shape Chapter 4's Redis cache already handles well. The genuinely hard part isn't storage, it's write amplification: one post from an account with 10,000 followers means 10,000 individual feed-list updates, which is precisely why this needs Chapter 7's asynchronous workers — fanning out synchronously inside the post-creation request would recreate 7.1's exact blocking problem.

Ranking: feeds aren't just chronological anymore

A purely chronological feed is simple but not what most real platforms show — ranking (engagement prediction, recency, relationship strength) requires either a separate scoring pass at read time or storing additional signal alongside each feed entry. This is a genuinely deep rabbit hole on its own (ranking model design, feature engineering) — worth naming as a real, separate concern in an interview, without needing to fully solve it unless specifically asked to go deeper there.

Check your understanding

1. What's the core trade-off between fan-out on write (push) and fan-out on read (pull)?
This is the fundamental design tension the whole topic revolves around — there's no universally better option, only a better fit for a given account's follower pattern.
2. Why does a hybrid push-for-most/pull-for-celebrities approach solve both extremes better than either pure strategy?
This is a direct, explicit callback to an earlier chapter's pattern applied to a new problem — exactly the kind of pattern recognition this track has been building toward.
3. Why must fanning out a post to thousands of followers' feeds happen asynchronously, rather than inside the post-creation request itself?
This directly reuses Chapter 7.2's message queue and background worker pattern — recognizing the same shape of problem (slow, non-urgent work blocking a fast response) in a new context.
