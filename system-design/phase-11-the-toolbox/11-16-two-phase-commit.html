11.16 Two-phase commit

Chapter 8.3 solved cross-service consistency with sagas. Here's the more "obvious" answer nearly everyone asks about first — and precisely why it's not what Chirp actually used.

TL;DR
Two-phase commit (2PC) tries to make a multi-service operation genuinely atomic, the way a single-database transaction is (11.9): a coordinator asks every participant to prepare (do the work, but don't finalize it — stay ready to either commit or roll back), and only once every participant confirms it's prepared does the coordinator tell everyone to commit. This sounds like exactly what 8.3 needed. The reason Chirp used sagas instead: 2PC is blocking — if the coordinator crashes between the prepare and commit messages, every participant is stuck holding locks, unable to proceed, until the coordinator recovers.

Why this is worse than it sounds for a system shaped like Chirp's

While a participant is "prepared" but waiting for the final commit instruction, it typically holds a lock on the relevant data — meaning nothing else can touch that row until the coordinator resolves the transaction. Recall Chapter 10.1's cascading failure lesson: a coordinator crash under 2PC doesn't just fail one operation, it can leave real database locks held indefinitely across multiple services, exactly the kind of resource-holding failure mode that chapter identified as dangerous at scale.

Why sagas won the trade-off for Chirp's account deletion

8.3's saga accepts a real cost — a brief window where the system is genuinely, visibly inconsistent (a post deleted but not yet reflected in search) — in exchange for never blocking indefinitely. Each step in a saga commits immediately and independently; if a later step fails, compensating actions clean up rather than everyone waiting on a coordinator. For a social app where a few seconds of visible inconsistency is a real but tolerable cost, this is a better trade than the availability risk 2PC's blocking nature introduces.

Where 2PC still genuinely earns its place

2PC (or protocols built on similar ideas) remains the right choice specifically when true atomicity is worth the availability cost — the exact "consistency over availability" choice named in 11.3's CAP framework and demonstrated concretely in 13.4's payment system, where briefly blocking is a far better outcome than a customer being charged without receiving what they paid for.
