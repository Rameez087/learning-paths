11.8 The system design interview framework

Ten chapters of Chirp's story, informally. Here's the same process, formalized into the four-step structure a real 45-60 minute interview actually rewards.

TL;DR
Clarify (~10%): ask about scope, scale, and edge cases before designing anything — this is graded, not preamble. Decompose (~20%): produce a defensible high-level architecture, 5-8 components, before drilling into any one of them — exactly Chirp's "meet the whole system" chapter openings. Deep-dive (~60%): protect this time for the one or two hardest problems (a specific bottleneck, a specific trade-off) rather than spreading shallow attention everywhere — this is where Chirp's actual "bottleneck → fix → new problem" pattern happens. Evaluate (~10%): name trade-offs and failure modes explicitly, the way every "New Problem" page in this track did.

Try it: see how the time budget actually breaks down

Why "clarify" is graded, not just polite preamble

Recall Chapter 1.1's framing: "design Chirp" could mean ten different systems depending on scope, scale, and edge cases. A candidate who immediately starts drawing boxes is skipping a step interviewers explicitly watch for — the ability to scope an ambiguous problem before solving it. This maps directly onto how this whole track's discovery worked implicitly: every chapter started by understanding the actual current pain before reaching for a fix.

Why deep-dive time is protected, specifically

A candidate who spends equal time on every component of a design ends up shallow everywhere — the weaker pattern. A candidate who identifies the hardest 1-2 problems (say, "how do we handle the celebrity/hot-shard problem" from 6.4, or "how do we keep the feed fast while staying consistent" from 5.3) and genuinely goes deep there demonstrates real understanding. This is precisely why this track dedicated entire chapters to specific hard problems — Chapter 6's consistent hashing, Chapter 8's saga pattern — rather than a shallow survey of every concept in equal measure.

Evaluate: stating trade-offs before being asked

Every single "New Problem" page in this entire track modeled this exact behavior — after proposing a fix, immediately and honestly naming what it costs. Chapter 4.2 didn't wait to be asked "what happens if a user edits and sees stale data" — it made explicit the trade-off being made. Doing this unprompted in an actual interview is precisely the "name failure modes before being asked" signal that distinguishes stronger candidates.
