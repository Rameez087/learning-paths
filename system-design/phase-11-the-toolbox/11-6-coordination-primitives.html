11.6 Coordination primitives

"What happens when a node dies?" has come up constantly throughout this track without a shared vocabulary. Here's the vocabulary.

TL;DR
Heartbeats: nodes periodically signal "I'm alive" — missing heartbeats is how a system detects a failure (exactly what 3.3's health checks and 10.2's circuit breakers rely on). Leader election: when multiple nodes could coordinate a task, one is elected "leader" to avoid conflicting decisions — if the leader stops sending heartbeats, another node is elected automatically. Quorum: requiring agreement from a majority of nodes (not all of them) before considering an operation successful — tolerates some nodes being down while still guaranteeing consistency.

Try it: watch leader election happen after a failure

Heartbeats: the mechanism underneath most failure detection in this track

Recall Chapter 3.3's load balancer health checks and Chapter 5.2's replicas — both rely on the same underlying idea: periodic signals confirming a node is alive and responsive. Missing a configured number of consecutive heartbeats is what triggers "this node is down" — the load balancer removes it from rotation, a replica gets flagged for investigation, a circuit breaker (10.2) trips. Nearly every failure-detection mechanism in this entire track ultimately rests on this one simple primitive.

Why leader election specifically matters for Chirp's sharded, replicated setup

Recall Chapter 5.2: writes go to exactly one primary database per shard. If that primary fails, something needs to promote one of its replicas to become the new primary — and critically, all the other replicas need to agree on which one, or the system risks ending up with two machines both believing they're the primary (a genuinely dangerous "split-brain" scenario). Leader election protocols exist specifically to make this promotion happen safely and unambiguously.

Quorum: why "majority," not "everyone," is usually the right bar

Requiring literally every replica to confirm a write before considering it successful would mean one single slow or failed replica blocks all writes entirely — far too fragile. Requiring only a majority (a quorum) means the system tolerates some nodes being down or slow while still guaranteeing that any two quorums necessarily overlap by at least one node — which is what prevents two conflicting decisions from both being independently confirmed as "successful" at the same time.
