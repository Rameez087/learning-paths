11.14 Percentile literacy: p50, p95, p99

Every incident banner in this track quoted a p95 or p99 number without ever explaining what that actually means. Time to fix that.

TL;DR
The average response time hides exactly the thing that matters most: how bad the worst requests actually are. A percentile answers a sharper question — "p95 = 400ms" means 95% of requests finished in 400ms or less, and 5% took longer. Engineers obsess over p99 (or even p999) specifically because average can look perfectly healthy while a meaningful slice of real users are having a genuinely bad experience — and at scale, "rare" still means thousands of people.

Try it: watch average lie while p99 tells the truth

Why p99, not p100 (the true maximum)

The absolute slowest request ever recorded is usually a genuine fluke — a one-off network blip, not representative of anything systemic. p99 is chosen as a practical, stable cutoff: extreme enough to reveal real tail problems, but not so extreme that a single freak event dominates the number. Chapter 10.1's cascading failure detection cared about exactly this signal — a service whose p99 suddenly spikes while its average barely moves is showing early symptoms of exactly the kind of slow-not-down problem that chapter described.

Why tail latency compounds at scale — the multi-request page problem

If a single request has a 1% chance of hitting a genuinely slow p99 case, that sounds rare. But a single Chirp page load might trigger 20 separate backend calls (feed items, profile data, notification counts). The probability that at least one of those 20 calls hits the slow 1% case is far higher than 1% — this is exactly why services with many internal dependencies (Chapter 8's microservices) need to obsess over tail latency specifically, not just average performance per call.

Setting SLOs on percentiles, not averages

A service-level objective stated as "average response time under 200ms" can be satisfied while a real fraction of users wait several seconds. A percentile-based SLO — "p99 under 500ms" — makes an actual commitment about the worst realistic experience, which is what users and downstream services calling this one (Chapter 10's cascading failure risk) actually care about.

Check your understanding

1. Why can average response time look healthy even when a meaningful number of real users are having a bad experience?
This is the core reason percentiles matter — averages are mathematically dominated by the bulk of fast requests, hiding exactly the tail behavior that percentiles are designed to reveal.
2. Why does tail latency (like a 1% chance of a slow p99 request) become a much bigger practical problem when a single page load triggers many backend calls?
This is a genuinely important, often-underappreciated consequence — tail latency compounds across a request fan-out, making it a much bigger deal for microservice architectures than for simple single-call systems.
3. Why is a percentile-based SLO ("p99 under 500ms") a stronger commitment than an average-based one ("average under 200ms")?
This is the practical, operational payoff of understanding percentiles — it directly changes what kind of commitment a team is actually making to their users and to services depending on them.

Sources: Kleppmann & Riccomini, Designing Data-Intensive Applications, 2nd ed. (O'Reilly, 2026), Chapter 2 "Defining Nonfunctional Requirements" — latency, response time, and the use of average/median/percentile metrics.
