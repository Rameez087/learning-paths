11.15 Sharding by key range & secondary indexes

Chapter 6.2 sharded Chirp by hashing user_id. There's a genuinely different way to split data — and a real complication hash sharding quietly sidesteps.

TL;DR
Range sharding assigns contiguous key ranges to each shard (users A-F on shard 1, G-M on shard 2, and so on) — unlike Chapter 6.2's hash-based approach, this keeps related keys physically close, making range queries ("all posts between March and June") genuinely fast. The cost: range sharding is far more prone to hot spots, since real-world keys are rarely evenly distributed across the alphabet or timestamp range. Separately: once data is sharded, a secondary index (indexing something other than the shard key, like "posts containing #hashtag") can be local (each shard indexes only its own data — fast writes, expensive scatter-gather reads) or global (one shared index across all shards — fast reads, expensive distributed writes).

Try it: compare hash vs. range sharding for different query types

Why Chapter 6.2's hash sharding was still the right call for Chirp

Chirp's dominant queries are almost entirely "get this specific user's data" — a single-key lookup, not a range scan. Hash sharding's even distribution (helped further by 6.3's consistent hashing) was exactly right for that access pattern, and it's precisely what avoided the hot-spot risk range sharding would have introduced for arbitrary user IDs. Range sharding earns its place specifically when range queries are actually common — a time-series system where "give me all events from last Tuesday" is the dominant query shape, for instance.

The secondary index trade-off, made concrete with hashtags

Suppose Chirp adds hashtag search — "find all posts tagged #launch." A local secondary index means each of the 8 shards maintains its own hashtag index for only the posts it holds; writing a new hashtagged post is fast (touches one shard), but finding all #launch posts requires querying all 8 shards and merging results — exactly 6.4's scatter-gather problem, reappearing for a new reason. A global secondary index means one shared index mapping hashtag → post IDs across every shard; reads become a single fast lookup, but a write now has to update both the local shard and the global index — a small, real instance of 8.3's cross-shard consistency problem, on every single post.

Why there's no universally correct choice here

Local indexes favor write speed and simplicity at the cost of read fan-out; global indexes favor read speed at the cost of write complexity and consistency risk. The right choice depends on Chirp's actual read/write ratio for hashtag search specifically — the same "check the dominant access pattern first" discipline from 11.4's database taxonomy, applied one layer deeper, to index placement within an already-sharded system.

Check your understanding

1. Why is range sharding more prone to hot spots than the hash sharding Chirp actually used in Chapter 6.2?
This is the genuine trade-off driving the choice between the two schemes — hash sharding trades away range-query efficiency specifically to get this even-distribution property.
2. Why does a local secondary index for hashtags require querying every shard to find all posts with a given hashtag?
This is a direct, explicit reappearance of 6.4's cross-shard search cost, now shown to be a general property of local secondary indexes, not just a one-off search-feature problem.
3. What does a global secondary index trade away in exchange for fast reads?
This is the genuine, symmetric trade-off — fast reads in exchange for write complexity and consistency risk, directly connecting to the saga-pattern challenge already covered in 8.3.
