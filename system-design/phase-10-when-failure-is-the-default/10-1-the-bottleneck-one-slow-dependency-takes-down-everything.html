10.1 The bottleneck: one slow dependency takes down everything

The Notifications service got slow — not down, just slow. Twenty minutes later, the entire Chirp platform was unreachable, including services that never even talk to Notifications directly.

⚠ Alert
Notifications Service p99 latency climbed from 40ms to 12 seconds after a downstream email provider started timing out. Within 20 minutes, Feed Service, Profile Service, and the API gateway all became unresponsive — a single slow dependency cascaded into a total platform outage.
"This is the scariest incident we've had. Notifications wasn't even down — it was just slow. But every service that calls it kept threads waiting on those slow calls, ran out of available threads for anything else, and started timing out themselves. Then services that called THOSE services started failing too. One slow dependency, and twenty minutes later the whole platform is dark." — you, in the postmortem
TL;DR
At Chirp's current scale, with dozens of interdependent services (Chapter 8) calling each other constantly, a single slow — not even fully down — dependency can trigger a cascading failure: callers wait on slow responses, exhaust their own limited resources (threads, connections) waiting, become slow or unresponsive themselves, and pass that same failure mode on to whoever calls them. This is a genuinely new category of risk that microservices specifically introduced — a monolith couldn't cascade quite this way, because there was no network call chain for a slowdown to propagate across.

Try it: watch one slow service cascade through the system

Why "slow" is genuinely more dangerous than "down"

A service that's fully down fails fast — callers get an immediate error and can react (retry elsewhere, show a fallback, whatever the situation calls for). A service that's merely slow is more insidious: callers keep waiting, holding onto resources (a thread, a connection) for the full timeout duration, for every single request, before eventually failing. Under real traffic volume, this resource exhaustion happens far faster than most teams expect — and it's exactly why "slow" deserves to be treated as seriously as "down," not as a lesser problem.

Why this is the natural, inevitable next chapter after Chapter 8

Every service added since Chapter 8's split is another dependency some other part of the system relies on, and another potential source of a cascading failure. This isn't a flaw in the microservices decision — it's the honest, deferred cost of that trade-off finally coming due at real scale. Earlier in the track, failure modes were mostly contained to one component; at this scale, with this many interdependent services, failure needs to be treated as the default expectation, not a rare exception to plan for after the fact.
