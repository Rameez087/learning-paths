2.1 Tokenization & byte-pair encoding

The step before the model ever sees your text — and why it's not splitting on spaces.

TL;DR
LLMs don't tokenize by word or by character — they use subword tokenization, almost always a variant of Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE). It starts with individual characters and repeatedly merges whichever adjacent pair appears most often in the training data, building up a vocabulary of common chunks. Frequent words end up as a single token; rare words get broken into recognizable pieces. This is why token count for the same sentence can vary between models, and why weird or foreign words sometimes cost far more tokens than you'd expect.

Why not just split on spaces or characters?

Three tokenization strategies exist, and each fails for a different reason:

  • Word-level — one token per word. Vocabulary explodes to hundreds of thousands of entries, and any word the model didn't see during training (typos, new slang, rare technical terms) becomes an unhandleable "unknown" token.
  • Character-level — one token per letter. No vocabulary problem, but sequences become extremely long, and individual characters carry almost no meaning on their own — the model has to work much harder to reconstruct words from letters.
  • Subword (BPE) — the middle ground. Common whole words become single tokens; rare or unfamiliar words get broken into meaningful pieces the model has seen before. "boys" might split into "boy" + "s", letting the model reuse what it already knows about "boy."

What this means in practice

  • Token count ≠ word count or character count. This is why API pricing (billed per token) doesn't map cleanly onto "per word" intuitions.
  • Different models, different vocabularies. The same sentence can tokenize into a different number of tokens depending on which model's tokenizer processes it — each model family trains its own BPE vocabulary on its own data mix.
  • Non-English text and code often cost more tokens per unit of content, if the tokenizer's training data was mostly English prose — the vocabulary simply has fewer single-token shortcuts for other languages or symbol-heavy code.
Reference
Neural Machine Translation of Rare Words with Subword Units — Sennrich, Haddow & Birch, 2015. Adapted the original 1994 Byte-Pair Encoding data-compression algorithm (Philip Gage) into the subword tokenization method that underlies most modern LLM tokenizers.

Check your understanding

1. Why doesn't BPE ever need an "unknown word" token the way word-level tokenization does?
BPE's vocabulary includes individual characters as a fallback, so worst case, an unseen word can always be represented as a sequence of known smaller pieces, right down to single characters if needed.
2. What determines which pair of symbols BPE merges at each step during training?
BPE is a greedy, frequency-driven algorithm — at each step it counts adjacent pairs across the corpus and merges the single most common one, without any built-in linguistic knowledge of what a "real" prefix or suffix is.
3. Why might the same sentence use more tokens in one model's tokenizer than another's?
Tokenization happens before the transformer even runs, using a vocabulary trained separately for each model family — different training corpora produce different merge rules and therefore different token counts for the same text.

Sources: Sennrich, Haddow & Birch (2015); Sebastian Raschka's tokenization & BPE reference; GeeksforGeeks and Towards Data Science BPE explainers.
