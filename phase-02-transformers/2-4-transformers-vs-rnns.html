2.4 Why transformers replaced RNNs

A hardware story as much as a math story.

TL;DR
Before transformers, sequence models (RNNs, LSTMs) processed tokens one at a time, in order — each step's computation literally could not start until the previous step finished. Transformers process every token in parallel, which is a perfect match for how GPUs actually work. That single architectural change is a big part of why models scaled from hundreds of millions of parameters to over a trillion in less than a decade — the hardware finally stopped fighting the software.

Two separate problems, one architectural fix

RNNs had two distinct failures, and it's worth keeping them separate because transformers fix them for two different reasons:

  • Speed: no parallelization. An RNN's hidden state at step t is a function of the hidden state at step t−1. Step 5 mathematically cannot begin until step 4 has finished. Modern GPUs are built to do enormous numbers of independent calculations simultaneously — forcing them through a strictly sequential chain wastes almost all of that capability. Transformers reduce this to effectively a single parallel step per layer, since no token's attention computation depends on another token's computation finishing first.
  • Quality: vanishing gradients over long sequences. During training, correcting the model's mistakes requires propagating a gradient signal backward through every step. In an RNN, that signal has to pass through every intermediate step, and repeated multiplication through many steps tends to shrink the signal toward zero (or occasionally explode). Practically, this meant RNNs got progressively worse at connecting information across long spans of text — exactly the "murderer was" long-range problem from 1.2. Attention sidesteps this because every token connects directly to every other token, with no intermediate steps for the signal to degrade through.

The trade-off transformers introduced

This isn't a free lunch. Solving the sequential bottleneck introduced a different one: attention compares every token against every other token, so its computational cost grows quadratically with sequence length — double the input length and the attention computation roughly quadruples. This is the direct cause of the context-window cost curve we'll dig into in 2.5, and it's an active area of architecture research (sparse attention, state-space models like Mamba, and hybrid architectures all exist specifically to attack this trade-off).

Check your understanding

1. Why can't an RNN's computation be parallelized across time steps the way a transformer's can?
The recurrence relation itself creates the bottleneck — step t needs the output of step t−1 to even begin, which is a structural dependency, not a hardware limitation.
2. What causes the vanishing gradient problem in RNNs?
Attention heads are a transformer concept, not an RNN one — the vanishing gradient problem is specific to backpropagating error signals through long sequential chains of computation.
3. What trade-off did transformers introduce in exchange for solving the sequential bottleneck?
Because every token attends to every other token, cost scales roughly with the square of sequence length — this is exactly why longer context windows get disproportionately more expensive, covered next in 2.5.
