2.4 Debugging under pressure

A VP is watching. The system is down. Panic is the natural response — and the exact thing that makes debugging take longer.

TL;DR
Debugging under customer pressure needs a method that survives adrenaline, because your normal unhurried debugging instincts degrade under stress. The core discipline: separate containment (stop the bleeding) from root cause analysis (understand why) — don't let the pressure to explain "why" delay the actions that limit damage right now. This exact sequencing shows up again in Phase 5's incident response material, because it's the same underlying skill applied at a bigger scale.

Try it: work through a live incident, step by step

Why "reproduce it locally first" is often the wrong instinct under pressure

Your default instinct — pull the code down, try to reproduce it on your own machine — can burn twenty minutes while the customer's system stays broken. Under real time pressure, look first at what changed most recently (a deploy, a config change, a data update) and what the actual error output says, rather than starting from "let me recreate this from scratch." Recent-change correlation solves a large share of production incidents faster than full reproduction does.

Narrating out loud: a genuinely useful habit, not just for show

Saying your hypothesis out loud as you test it — "I think this is a connection pool exhaustion issue, checking pool metrics now" — does two things at once: it keeps the customer informed (which reduces their own panic and lets them course-correct if you're chasing the wrong thing), and it forces you to actually articulate your reasoning, which catches faulty logic faster than silent debugging does. This becomes a core skill again in Phase 9's stakeholder communication material, but it starts here, at the terminal.

The specific trap: fixing the symptom instead of the cause under time pressure

Restarting a hung service makes the immediate symptom disappear — and is often the right first move for containment. But if that's where the incident response stops, the same failure returns, usually at a worse time. A disciplined process explicitly separates "what did we do to stop the bleeding" from "what did we determine actually caused it, and did we fix that root cause too" — treating a restart as containment, not resolution.

Check your understanding

1. Why should containment be separated from root cause analysis during a live incident?
This sequencing — stop the bleeding first, understand why second — prevents the search for a full explanation from delaying action that could limit ongoing damage.
2. Why might checking recent changes be faster than attempting to reproduce an issue locally under time pressure?
This isn't a claim that reproduction is worthless — it's a time-pressure prioritization: correlating with recent changes often gets to an answer faster when minutes matter.
3. Why is restarting a hung service considered containment rather than resolution?
This distinction matters practically — treating a restart as "done" skips the root-cause step, setting up a repeat incident later unless the actual cause gets investigated separately.
