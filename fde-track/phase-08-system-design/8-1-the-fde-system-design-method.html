8.1 The FDE system design method

A generic system design interview asks "design Twitter." An FDE design conversation asks "design this, for this specific customer, inside these specific constraints, and explain your tradeoffs to both the engineer and the VP in the room."

TL;DR
FDE system design differs from a standard interview-style exercise in one critical way: the constraints aren't hypothetical, they're the specific customer's actual environment — everything from Phase 6's enterprise integration reality, Phase 4's cloud/networking limitations, and Phase 7's AI security requirements all apply simultaneously, for one real deployment. The method below is what the next three topics apply to three concrete scenarios.

Try it: walk through the method

Why "requirements" means something more specific here than in a generic interview

A textbook system design exercise often invents its own requirements as it goes. An FDE engagement's requirements come from actual discovery (Phase 9) — a real customer's real latency tolerance, real data sensitivity classification, real existing infrastructure. The discipline here: resist designing before discovery is genuinely done, even under time pressure, since a technically elegant design built on wrong assumptions about the customer's actual constraints is worse than a rougher design built on accurate ones.

Presenting tradeoffs to two audiences in the same room

A specifically FDE-flavored skill: the same design conversation often has both a technical counterpart (who wants to understand the actual architecture and its failure modes) and a business stakeholder (who wants to understand cost, timeline, and risk) present at once. The practical technique: state the tradeoff once, in plain terms ("we can ship faster with less redundancy, or slower with a fully redundant setup — here's what each costs and risks"), which genuinely serves both audiences rather than requiring two separate explanations.

Constraints stack, they don't average

A common mistake: treating multiple real constraints (data must stay on-premise, latency must be under 200ms, the budget caps at a specific compute tier) as things to balance against each other, when they're often all simultaneously hard requirements that must each be fully satisfied. The design method's job is finding an architecture that satisfies every genuine constraint at once — not a compromise that partially violates each one a little.

Check your understanding

1. Why do FDE system design constraints differ fundamentally from a typical system design interview's constraints?
This is the core distinction the whole phase is built around — real constraints from real discovery, not invented ones, which is exactly why Phase 9's discovery work has to genuinely precede design.
2. Why is stating a tradeoff once, in plain terms, often better than giving separate technical and business explanations to different audiences in the same room?
This is a practical communication efficiency — a well-chosen plain-language framing of a real tradeoff doesn't require dumbing anything down, and it keeps both audiences in the same shared understanding.
3. Why should multiple real customer constraints often be treated as things that must all be simultaneously satisfied, rather than balanced against each other?
This is the "constraints stack, don't average" principle — a design that's 80% compliant with a hard data-residency requirement is still non-compliant, not 80% acceptable.

This method synthesizes principles from earlier phases: discovery-driven requirements (Phase 9), real infrastructure constraints (Phases 4 and 6), and the dual-audience communication skill developed further in Phase 9.2.
