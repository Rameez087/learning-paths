5.3 SLIs, SLOs & incident response

"Two bad rollouts burned 88% of a 30-day error budget in a day, while the SLI still read 99.56%." A healthy-looking number can hide a genuine crisis in progress.

TL;DR
An SLI (Service Level Indicator) is a measured value — request success rate, latency. An SLO (Service Level Objective) is your internal target for that SLI — "99.9% of requests succeed." An SLA (Service Level Agreement) is the external contract with consequences if the SLO isn't met — and current practice recommends setting internal SLOs stricter than external SLAs, as a safety buffer. The gap between perfect (100%) and your SLO is your error budget — and alerting on how fast you're burning that budget, not just the raw SLI, is what catches a real crisis while the current snapshot still looks fine.

Why burn-rate alerting beats simple threshold alerting

A fast burn (2% of budget in 1 hour) deserves paging someone immediately — P0, 5-minute response. A slow burn (10% over 3 days) might only need next-business-day attention. A simple "alert if error rate exceeds X%" threshold can't distinguish these two very different situations, while burn-rate alerting fires specifically when you're consuming reliability budget faster than sustainable — catching a real crisis while you still have budget left to respond deliberately, rather than after it's already gone.

Blameless postmortems: the culture that makes incident response actually work

A specific, repeated principle: focus accountability on systems and processes, not individuals. "Why didn't our CI catch this?" instead of "why didn't you test properly?" This isn't just a nicety — a team that fears blame for an honest mistake starts hiding information during incidents, which is exactly the information a genuine root-cause investigation needs. In an FDE context specifically, this culture matters doubly: you're often the outsider in the room during an incident, and how you handle blame directly shapes whether the customer's team trusts you enough to share what actually happened.

What a good postmortem actually contains

  • Timeline — when the incident started, when it was detected, when it was resolved.
  • Impact — who was affected, how badly, for how long — ideally tied to actual SLI data, not just impressions.
  • Root cause — the actual mechanism, not just the symptom that was fixed.
  • Action items — specific, owned, and tracked — not vague intentions that quietly never happen.

Check your understanding

1. Why is burn-rate alerting more useful than a simple fixed-threshold alert?
The rate of consumption carries information a static threshold can't — the same current error rate could represent a genuine crisis or a manageable blip depending on how fast it's actually burning the budget.
2. Why does current guidance recommend setting internal SLOs stricter than external SLAs?
This buffer is a deliberate risk-management choice — internal alarm bells ring before the external, consequence-bearing threshold is actually crossed.
3. Why does blameless postmortem culture matter specifically for an FDE embedded with a customer during an incident?
Trust and information flow are directly connected — a team that fears blame withholds exactly the details a real investigation needs, and an outsider's handling of that dynamic matters even more than an insider's.
