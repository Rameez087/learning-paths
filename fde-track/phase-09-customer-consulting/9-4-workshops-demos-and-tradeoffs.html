9.4 Workshops, demos & tradeoffs

The demo will break in front of the room eventually. The question isn't whether — it's whether you've prepared for that moment.

TL;DR
A technical workshop and a demo serve different purposes and need different preparation. A workshop is collaborative — the goal is surfacing information and building shared understanding (directly extending 9.1's discovery skills into a group setting). A demo is performative — the goal is building confidence in a specific outcome, which means anticipating failure modes matters as much as showcasing what works.

When the demo breaks live

Why a demo script needs a "plan B" branch, not just a happy path

A live demo depends on a network connection, an API being up, and data behaving as expected — any one of which can fail at the worst possible moment. The specific discipline: have a recorded backup video or screenshot sequence ready, know exactly which parts of the demo are most fragile (usually anything depending on live network calls) and have a graceful verbal fallback ready ("let me show you what this looks like when it's working — here's a recording from this morning's test run") rather than visibly panicking or trying to debug live in front of the room.

Presenting tradeoffs live, connecting back to 8.1 and 9.2

A workshop or demo is often exactly where 8.1's "state the tradeoff once, plainly" and 9.2's audience-translation skills get used in real time, live, with both technical and business people in the room simultaneously. The preparation that actually pays off: rehearsing how to state the two or three most likely tradeoff questions plainly, in advance, rather than improvising a business-friendly explanation for the first time under pressure.

Workshops: getting the room to actually talk, not just listen

A workshop that's really just a presentation with a different name wastes its main advantage — real-time interaction. Specific techniques that actually get a room contributing: asking a direct, answerable question early (not "any questions?" but "what does this process actually look like on your end today?"), deliberately pausing after asking rather than filling silence immediately, and explicitly inviting disagreement ("does this match what you're seeing, or is something different?") rather than presenting conclusions as already settled.

Check your understanding

1. What's the fundamental difference in purpose between a workshop and a demo?
This distinction is why they need genuinely different preparation — a workshop's success depends on drawing out the room's input, while a demo's success depends on confidently showing a specific, prepared outcome.
2. Why should a demo's preparation include a "plan B" branch rather than just rehearsing the happy path?
This is a realistic acknowledgment that live technical dependencies can fail regardless of preparation quality — the professionalism is in the recovery, not in avoiding failure entirely.
3. Why does asking "what does this process look like on your end today?" work better as a workshop-opening question than "any questions?"
This is a practical facilitation technique — a concrete, answerable prompt lowers the barrier to speaking up compared to an open-ended invitation that's easy to decline silently.
