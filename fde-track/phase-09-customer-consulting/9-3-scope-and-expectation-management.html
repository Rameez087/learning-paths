9.3 Scope & expectation management

"Can you also just add..." is the four most expensive words in any engagement, and saying yes reflexively is how a two-week project becomes a two-month one.

TL;DR
Scope creep rarely arrives as one dramatic ask — it arrives as a series of individually reasonable-sounding small additions, each easy to say yes to in isolation. The discipline isn't refusing every request; it's making the tradeoff visible every time: "yes, we can add that — here's what it means for the timeline or what else it would displace." Saying no well is a skill, not just a boundary.

Try it: respond to a scope-creep request

The situation: Three days before a scheduled demo, the customer's stakeholder asks: "Since we're already in there, can you also add export-to-PDF? Shouldn't take long, right?"

Why the third response is specifically the right shape

It doesn't reflexively agree (which risks silently absorbing the cost and quietly slipping the original commitment) and it doesn't flatly refuse (which can feel unhelpful and doesn't actually serve the customer's real interest). It makes the tradeoff explicit and hands the actual decision to the person who should be making it — the stakeholder, who has context on which outcome matters more to them that the FDE doesn't have.

The "just" trap

Requests that include the word "just" ("can you just add...", "it's just a small change") are worth specific attention — "just" is doing a lot of work minimizing what's actually being asked, often unintentionally. A quick mental check: would this request, described without the word "just," still sound small? If describing it plainly reveals real scope, treat it as real scope, regardless of how it was originally phrased.

Documenting scope changes as they happen, not retroactively

When a scope change is agreed to, writing it down immediately — what changed, why, and what it costs — prevents the specific, common dispute that happens weeks later: "I don't remember agreeing that this would delay things." A brief written confirmation (even just a short email recap) protects both the FDE and the customer relationship by keeping the record accurate as changes actually happen, not reconstructed from memory afterward.

Check your understanding

1. Why does scope creep typically arrive as a series of small requests rather than one large one?
This is exactly why the discipline of making every tradeoff explicit matters — the danger isn't any one request, it's the pattern of reflexively accepting each one without tracking the total.
2. Why is making the tradeoff explicit and handing the decision to the stakeholder better than either always saying yes or always saying no?
This is the specific shape of a good response to scope requests — transparency about cost plus respecting the stakeholder's authority to make the actual tradeoff call.
3. Why should the word "just" in a scope request trigger extra scrutiny rather than being taken at face value?
This is a practical linguistic tell worth noticing — the word itself can obscure genuine scope, which is why testing the request's plain-language size is a useful habit.
