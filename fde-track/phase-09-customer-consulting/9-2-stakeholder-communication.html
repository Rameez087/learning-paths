9.2 Stakeholder communication

"The professionals who move fastest are the ones who can translate technical work into a business outcome a non-technical stakeholder can act on." One sentence, and most of consulting communication follows from it.

TL;DR
The same underlying fact needs a genuinely different presentation depending on who's hearing it — not a different fact, a different framing. This directly extends 8.1's "state the tradeoff once, in plain terms" principle: a well-chosen plain-language framing often serves multiple audiences at once, rather than requiring an entirely separate explanation for each.

Written communication: status updates that don't require a meeting to interpret

A weak status update reports activity ("we worked on the integration this week"). A strong one reports state and implication ("the integration is 70% complete; the remaining 30% depends on the customer's security team approving our access request, submitted Tuesday — no action needed from you unless that approval doesn't land by Friday"). The second version lets a busy stakeholder actually understand where things stand and what, if anything, needs their attention — without a follow-up conversation.

Delivering bad news: the discipline of not burying it

A specifically important skill: when something is genuinely behind schedule or broken, saying so clearly and early, with a plan attached, rather than hoping it resolves before anyone notices. A customer who hears about a real problem from the FDE first, with a plan already in hand, trusts that FDE considerably more than one who has to ask "why didn't you tell us this was happening" after finding out some other way.

Technical writing as a durable artifact, not just a meeting summary

A design document or architecture writeup outlives the meeting where it was discussed — someone joining the project six months later, or a stakeholder who missed the original conversation, needs to be able to read it and understand the actual reasoning, not just the conclusion. This connects forward directly to Phase 1.3's Handoff stage: documentation written clearly enough for someone who wasn't in the room is part of what makes a system genuinely sustainable after the FDE moves on.

Check your understanding

1. What's the key difference between translating a technical fact for different audiences versus simply changing the fact itself?
This is the core translation skill — accuracy is preserved across audiences, only the presentation adapts to what's relevant and actionable for each one.
2. Why does a status update that reports "state and implication" serve a busy stakeholder better than one that reports "activity"?
The practical value is self-sufficiency — a good update answers the reader's real question ("do I need to do anything?") without them having to ask.
3. Why does delivering bad news early, with a plan attached, build more trust than waiting to see if the problem resolves on its own?
This is a specific, named trust-building discipline — proactive transparency about real problems, not just successes, is what distinguishes a genuinely trusted FDE relationship.
