Phase 6 · Enterprise integration

6.1 The enterprise environment mindset

Most enterprises discover they have two to three times more integrations than they thought they had. Nothing you assumed about "how systems are organized here" is safe to assume.

TL;DR
"The customer's environment is not clean" isn't a complaint — it's the actual job description. Twenty years of acquisitions, replatforming, and bolt-on tools accumulate real "integration debt": systems nobody fully understands anymore, integrations built by people who left the company years ago, and undocumented dependencies between things that look unrelated. The FDE's job is to work productively inside this reality, not to wait for it to be cleaned up first — that cleanup, if it ever happens, is a multi-year initiative, not a precondition for shipping.

Why integration debt accumulates specifically over decades

The pattern across real enterprise integration work: consolidation, not connection, is the actual first step. A company doesn't set out to have three overlapping identity systems and a homegrown message bus nobody remembers building — it accumulates through years of acquisitions bringing in their own tech stacks, platform migrations that never fully finished, and point-to-point integrations added under deadline pressure that were meant to be temporary. None of this is negligence in any single moment — it's the compound result of years of reasonable individual decisions.

The three-phase pattern that actually works

  1. Inventory and classify every integration in the estate before touching anything — most organizations are surprised by how much actually exists once someone counts.
  2. Consolidate the integration layer onto fewer, better-understood platforms, retiring point tools as you go rather than adding yet another one-off connection.
  3. Refactor business logic out of the integration code itself, back into the systems where it actually belongs — the unglamorous step that produces most of the long-term value, and the one most tempting to skip under deadline pressure.

Why "wait until it's cleaner" is never the right response

An engagement's timeline doesn't pause for the customer to fix their infrastructure first — that's frequently part of why an FDE is there at all. The practical discipline: work productively within real constraints, document what you learn about the mess as you go (this becomes genuinely valuable institutional knowledge the customer often doesn't have written down anywhere), and flag consolidation opportunities as a separate recommendation rather than blocking the current engagement on fixing them first.
