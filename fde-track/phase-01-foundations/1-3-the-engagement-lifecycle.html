1.3 The engagement lifecycle

Thirteen stages, and skipping any one of them is exactly where engagements go wrong.

TL;DR
A real FDE engagement moves through: Discover → Understand → Scope → Design → Prototype → Integrate → Build → Deploy → Observe → Iterate → Prove Impact → Scale → Handoff. This isn't a checklist to rush through — it's a dependency chain. Skipping Discover means Scope is guesswork. Skipping Observe means you can't Iterate on real signal. Skipping Prove Impact means the customer has no reason to renew, however well the system actually works. Every later phase in this track maps back onto one or more of these stages.

Try it: walk through every stage

The stage most engagements skip, and pay for later

Observe is the stage most commonly compressed or skipped under deadline pressure — teams ship and move to the next fire. But without genuine observability (Phase 5 of this track) into how the deployed system is actually behaving with real customer traffic, Iterate becomes guesswork instead of a response to real signal, and Prove Impact has no data to draw from. The stages aren't independent chores; each one is a prerequisite the next stage actually needs.

Discover and Scope are engineering work, not sales work

This is one of Palantir's original organizational patterns (1.1): customer discovery is treated as engineering work, done by the engineer who will actually build the system — not handed off from a separate sales or pre-sales function. The reasoning is direct: the person who has to build the thing is the person best positioned to ask the technical questions that reveal whether a proposed scope is actually buildable, and by when.

Handoff doesn't mean disappearing

"Handoff" in this lifecycle means transitioning the system to a stable, sustainable operating model — documentation, runbooks, a clear owner on the customer's side, monitoring in place — not simply walking away. A rushed or absent handoff is a common, specifically avoidable failure mode: the system that worked while the FDE was actively watching it can quietly degrade once nobody's paying close attention, exactly the kind of silent failure Phase 5's observability material is built to catch.

Check your understanding

1. Why is skipping the Observe stage under deadline pressure particularly costly later in the lifecycle?
This is the core "dependency chain" point — later stages genuinely need what earlier stages produce, so skipping one doesn't just remove that stage's value, it degrades everything downstream of it.
2. Why does Palantir's playbook treat customer discovery as engineering work rather than a separate sales function?
The reasoning is specifically about who has the right expertise to evaluate technical feasibility during discovery — the builder, not a separate function further from the implementation details.
3. What does "Handoff" actually mean in this lifecycle, as distinct from simply ending the engagement?
A rushed or absent handoff is specifically named as a failure mode — the goal is a system that survives and stays healthy after the FDE's direct attention moves elsewhere, not an abrupt exit.
