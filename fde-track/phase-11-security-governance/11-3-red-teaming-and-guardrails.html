11.3 Red teaming & guardrails

Controlled testing found full compromise of some agentic systems in under two hours. Finding this yourself, deliberately, beats a customer's security team finding it for you.

TL;DR
Red teaming means deliberately attacking your own system before deployment — attempting prompt injection, testing tenant isolation boundaries, trying to trigger excessive agency — to find weaknesses proactively rather than reactively. Guardrails are the runtime defenses that catch what red teaming reveals: input/output filtering, tool permission scoping, and monitoring for anomalous behavior. This closes the loop on everything from 7.2 and 11.1 by making those risks something you actively test for, not just something you're aware exists.

Why red teaming your own system matters more than a checklist review

A static checklist review ("do you have prompt injection defenses? yes/no") can be answered affirmatively without the defenses actually working under real adversarial pressure. Genuine red teaming — actually attempting the attacks, documenting what succeeded and what didn't — produces evidence rather than assertions, directly serving 7.3's evaluation-as-deliverable principle: showing a skeptical reviewer real, attempted attacks and their outcomes is far more credible than a checkbox confirming a policy exists.

Guardrails: defense that operates at runtime, not just design time

Even a red-teamed, well-designed system needs ongoing runtime defenses, since new attack patterns emerge after launch that testing couldn't have anticipated. Practical guardrail layers: input filtering (detecting known injection patterns before they reach the model), output filtering (catching a response that reveals something it shouldn't before it's returned to the user), and behavioral monitoring (flagging when an agent's actions deviate meaningfully from its normal pattern) — directly connecting to 7.2's six control layers and 5.2's observability material.

The specific discipline of documenting what you tested, and what you didn't

A genuinely useful red team report is explicit about scope: which attack categories were tested, which specific techniques were attempted, and — critically — what wasn't tested and why. A report that implies comprehensive coverage without stating its actual scope invites the exact kind of overconfidence 11.1's overreliance risk describes, just applied to the security review itself rather than the AI system's output.

Continuous red teaming, not a one-time pre-launch exercise

Recall 7.3's continuous evaluation principle: the same logic applies here. Attack techniques evolve, and a system's own behavior can drift as it's updated or as usage patterns change. A mature deployment includes periodic re-testing, not just a single pre-launch red team exercise treated as permanently sufficient.

Check your understanding

1. Why does genuine red teaming produce more credible evidence than a static checklist review?
This is the core distinction — demonstrated, tested resilience is a fundamentally stronger claim than an unverified assertion that defenses exist.
2. Why does a system still need runtime guardrails even after being thoroughly red-teamed before launch?
Pre-launch testing and ongoing runtime defense are complementary, not redundant — the security landscape keeps evolving after any point-in-time test.
3. Why is explicitly documenting what was NOT tested just as important as documenting what was tested in a red team report?
This is a specific honesty discipline — an implicitly overstated scope can mislead a reviewer into more confidence than the actual testing supports, echoing the overreliance risk in a different context.
