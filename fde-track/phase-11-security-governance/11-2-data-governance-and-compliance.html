11.2 Data governance & compliance

$12.9M a year — the Gartner-benchmarked cost large enterprises spend just on data governance and quality maintenance for AI workloads. This is not a rounding error in anyone's budget.

TL;DR
Data governance for AI deployments extends the Data Science track's governance material (contracts, lineage, ownership) with AI-specific requirements: knowing exactly what data fed a model or a RAG system's retrieval index, being able to demonstrate compliance with the specific regulatory framework governing that data (HIPAA, GDPR, SOX, depending on the industry from 10.3), and maintaining an audit trail robust enough to satisfy an external regulator, not just an internal reviewer.

Data lineage for AI: extending the Data Science track's principle

The Data Science & Engineering track's Phase 11 covers data lineage as a general warehouse practice — mapping which datasets feed which downstream tables. For AI deployments specifically, this extends to a harder question: which specific documents fed a RAG index, and can you trace a given model response back to the source documents that informed it? This matters directly for 7.3's evaluation-as-deliverable material — a compliance reviewer will often ask not just "is this accurate" but "can you prove exactly what data produced this specific answer."

Retention and deletion: the AI-specific complication

Standard data governance includes retention policies — how long data is kept, when it's deleted. AI systems complicate this: if a document was used to fine-tune a model, or is embedded in a vector index, "deleting" it from the source system doesn't automatically remove its influence from the model or the index. A genuinely compliant deletion process for an AI system needs to account for every place that data's influence persists, not just the original source record — a specific, real technical challenge with fine-tuned models and embedded indexes alike.

Consent and purpose limitation in an AI context

Data collected and consented to for one purpose (say, customer support ticket resolution) being repurposed to train or fine-tune a model is a genuinely distinct use that may not be covered by the original consent, depending on the applicable regulatory framework. This is a specific question worth raising proactively during discovery (9.1) with any customer proposing to use existing data for a new AI application — assuming existing consent automatically covers a new use case is a real, avoidable compliance risk.
