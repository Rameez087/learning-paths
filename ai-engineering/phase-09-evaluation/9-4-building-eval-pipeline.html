9.4 Building an eval pipeline

Seven items that ship in two weeks beat twenty items that never leave the planning doc.

TL;DR
Most "LLM evaluation best practices" guides are taxonomies, not checklists — comprehensive lists of every metric family and dashboard widget that nobody actually finishes building. A specific 2026 practitioner framework cuts this to seven items scoped to ship inside a single sprint. If you only build three this sprint: the golden dataset, a deterministic floor, and a CI gate — without those three, nothing else in the list can stand on its own.

Try it: the seven-item checklist

Why "the deterministic floor" comes before anything fancier

Before any LLM-as-judge scoring runs, a set of cheap, fast, deterministic checks (9.1) should catch obviously broken output — malformed JSON, wrong label enum, missing required fields. This "floor" is what prevents the more expensive parts of the pipeline from doing work on outputs that were already clearly broken, and it's specifically one of the three items flagged as load-bearing for everything else.

Why a CI gate matters even with a good dataset

A golden dataset sitting unused doesn't protect anything — the CI gate is what actually connects evaluation to the deployment process, blocking a regression from shipping rather than just measuring it after the fact. A dataset without a gate is a report; a dataset with a gate is a safeguard.

Putting the full phase together

This phase's four topics chain into each other directly: 9.1's golden dataset is what 9.2's judge gets calibrated against and what 9.3's offline evals run before every deployment; 9.3's online monitoring is what keeps 9.1's dataset current with real failures; and this checklist is the concrete, shippable version of assembling all three into something that actually runs, rather than a diagram of how they theoretically relate.

Check your understanding

1. According to the practical 2026 framework referenced above, which three items are load-bearing for the rest of an eval program?
These three are specifically flagged as what everything else depends on — without them, the rest of an evaluation program has nothing solid to stand on.
2. Why does a sprawling 20-item evaluation checklist often fail to ship, according to this practitioner framing?
The critique isn't that comprehensiveness is bad — it's that a checklist too large to action in a normal sprint tends to just get filed and ignored, which is worse than a smaller checklist that actually gets built.
3. Why is a CI gate necessary even if you already have a well-built golden dataset?
Measurement and enforcement are separate steps — a dataset that's never wired into the deployment pipeline is just a report generated after the fact, not a safeguard that prevents a regression from reaching users.
