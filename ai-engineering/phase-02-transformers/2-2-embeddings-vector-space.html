2.2 Embeddings & vector space

How "meaning" becomes a list of numbers a model can do math on.

TL;DR
An embedding turns a word (or sentence, image, anything) into a point in high-dimensional space — typically 300 to a few thousand numbers. Words with similar meaning end up as nearby points. "Nearby" is measured with cosine similarity: the angle between two vectors, ignoring their length. This is the mechanism underneath search, recommendation, and RAG retrieval — it's how a system finds "relevant" text without literal keyword matching.

Word analogies: the classic demonstration

The property that made embeddings famous: relationships between words show up as consistent directions in the vector space, not just clusters. The often-cited example is king − man + woman ≈ queen — the direction from "man" to "king" (something like "royalty") is approximately the same direction as from "woman" to "queen." This isn't hand-coded; it emerges purely from how often words co-occur across a massive amount of text.

Where embeddings actually get used

  • Search & retrieval (RAG, Phase 6): your document chunks and your query both get embedded, and the system retrieves whichever chunks are closest by cosine similarity — this is semantic search, matching meaning rather than exact keywords.
  • Recommendation: "similar items" often means "nearby in embedding space."
  • Clustering & classification: grouping similar documents, or training a lightweight classifier on top of embeddings instead of raw text.
  • Inside the transformer itself: every token is converted to an embedding before attention (Phase 1.2) ever runs — this is the "numbers in" step of the generation loop.

Why cosine similarity, not just distance

A natural instinct is to measure how "close" two points are with ordinary (Euclidean) distance — but that's sensitive to vector length, and vector length in embedding models often reflects something incidental, like how much text a document contains, not how related it is in meaning. Cosine similarity ignores length entirely and measures only the angle, which is why it became the standard metric for comparing embeddings: it isolates direction, and direction is where the semantic relationship lives.

Check your understanding

1. If you stretch vector A to be twice as long without changing its direction, what happens to its cosine similarity with vector B?
Cosine similarity measures only the angle between vectors, not their magnitude — stretching a vector along the same direction doesn't change the angle, so similarity is unaffected.
2. In RAG-style retrieval, what determines which document chunk gets retrieved for a query?
Semantic search embeds both the query and the stored chunks, then retrieves by vector similarity — this is how it can match relevant content even when the wording doesn't literally overlap.
3. What does the "king − man + woman ≈ queen" example demonstrate?
This relationship isn't hard-coded — it emerges from training on large text corpora, where the direction encoding a concept like "royalty" turns out to be consistent across related word pairs.
