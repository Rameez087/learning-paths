6.3 HyDE & query transformation

Sometimes the fix for bad retrieval isn't a better index — it's a better question.

TL;DR
HyDE (Hypothetical Document Embeddings) addresses a subtle mismatch: a short user question and a long, detailed document passage often don't embed close together in vector space, even when the passage perfectly answers the question. HyDE's fix: ask an LLM to generate a hypothetical answer to the question first, then embed and search using that — matching document-length text against document-length text. It measurably helps in some domains and measurably hurts in others, which makes it a genuinely conditional technique, not a universal upgrade.

Why the length mismatch matters

A question like "what causes cache invalidation issues in distributed systems" is short and abstract. A document passage that thoroughly answers it is long, specific, and full of concrete detail. Embedding models can genuinely struggle to recognize that a terse question and a verbose, detailed answer are "about the same thing," because their surface form is so different — this is the asymmetry HyDE targets directly.

The research finding that complicates the picture

HyDE's fix has a real failure mode: it works by generating a hypothetical answer with an LLM, and that hypothetical answer can be plausible-sounding but factually wrong — especially for domains that demand precise numbers or facts, like financial or scientific queries. One 2026 benchmark study found HyDE consistently underperforming plain dense retrieval specifically on financial question-answering, because the generated pseudo-document fabricated plausible-but-incorrect figures that pulled the search away from the actually correct answer. The recommendation drawn from this: avoid HyDE for domains where factual precision dominates over general semantic similarity.

Other query transformation techniques worth knowing

  • Multi-query — generate several reworded variants of the user's question, retrieve for each, and merge the results. One systematic study found this underperforming simple baseline retrieval, a reminder that "more sophisticated" doesn't automatically mean "better" — always measure against your own data.
  • Query decomposition — breaking a complex multi-part question into simpler sub-questions, retrieving separately for each, similar in spirit to the task decomposition covered in 3.5.

The consistent lesson across this research: retrieval-enhancement techniques are conditional improvements, not universal wins. The right default, per one recent benchmark's recommended decision framework, is to start with hybrid retrieval (BM25 + dense, fused) as a strong baseline, and only add HyDE, multi-query, or other transformations after measuring whether they actually help on your specific documents and queries.

Check your understanding

1. What problem is HyDE specifically designed to fix?
HyDE targets a length/form asymmetry — a terse question and a detailed answer can embed far apart even when perfectly relevant to each other, and HyDE closes that gap by embedding a hypothetical answer instead of the raw question.
2. Why did HyDE underperform on the financial question-answering benchmark mentioned above?
HyDE's mechanism depends on the hypothetical answer being reasonably close to correct — for domains demanding precise facts, a fabricated-but-plausible number can do more harm than the length mismatch HyDE was trying to fix.
3. What's the broader lesson from HyDE and multi-query's mixed research results?
Both techniques help in some studies and hurt in others depending on the domain — the practical takeaway is to start from a strong hybrid-retrieval baseline and empirically test any added technique rather than assuming it will help.
