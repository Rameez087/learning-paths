4.2 Baselines and ablations

A result means nothing until you say what it is better than. Baselines set the bar, and ablations show which part of your idea earned the gain.

TL;DR
Always compare against the simplest strong baseline that could explain your gain, tuned as carefully as your own method. Then run ablations: remove one component at a time and see what you lose. If a simple baseline matches your result, that is a finding worth reporting, not a failure.

Why simple baselines matter

Many published gains shrink or vanish when the baseline is tuned as hard as the new method. Lipton and Steinhardt describe, among other troubling trends, failure to identify the source of empirical gains. Starting with the simplest model that could do the job tells you how much headroom there is, and the comparison protects you from crediting a clever idea for what a good learning rate did.

Check your understanding

1. Why start with a simple baseline?
If a simple baseline matches your method, the extra complexity is not justified.
2. What does an ablation do?
Ablations attribute the improvement to specific components.
3. Which practice makes a comparison unfair?
Unequal tuning is a common way gains get inflated.
