9.2 Pitfalls clinic

Most first attempts at research fail in the same few ways. Practise recognising them before they cost you weeks.

TL;DR
The common traps: emailing before you have an artifact, sending the same text to everyone, using employer data in public work, chasing novelty, claiming gains without seeds, outsourcing your first read of a paper, reading silence as a no, relying on professors alone and missing deadlines while you build.

What went wrong?

You send 30 identical emails to professors in week 1. You get no replies.
Generic emails without evidence are easy to ignore.
Your reproduction beats the paper's number after you tuned your seed and settings against the test set.
Tuning on the test set and picking seeds inflates results (3.2, 4.4).
You ask a chatbot to summarise a paper and never read it. You cite its claims in your email and get one wrong.
Errors expose you immediately to experts. Do your own pass first.
You put a work notebook in your public repo as an example.
Employer code and data must not be published without permission.
You hear nothing for five days after emailing and conclude the answer is no.
Inboxes are busy. One polite follow up is normal.
You spend six months trying to invent a new architecture and have nothing to show.
Start with a finishable project and build up.

A short list to keep

  • Build the artifact before the outreach.
  • Make each email specific to that group.
  • Use public or synthetic data only in anything public.
  • Report seeds and intervals for every claim.
  • Do your own first read of every paper.
  • Follow up once after about a week.
  • Run open community routes in parallel.
  • List deadlines in week 4 and apply with work in progress if needed.

Check your understanding

1. What is the most common reason cold emails fail?
Replies go to emails that are specific and backed by an artifact.
2. What is a safe use of AI when reading papers?
AI is a helper after you have read, not a substitute.
3. Why should a reproduction be your first project?
Scoped, finishable work is the best starting point.

Sources: Troubling Trends in ML Scholarship (Lipton, Steinhardt); Improving Reproducibility in ML Research (Pineau et al.); How to Email a Potential Supervisor (Academic Positions)
