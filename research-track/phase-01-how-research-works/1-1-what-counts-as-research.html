1.1 What counts as research, and what a contribution is

Most people who want to do research have never been told what the finish line looks like. It is a claim, backed by evidence, that someone else can check.

TL;DR
Research is answering a question that has no good answer yet, in a way other people can verify. The unit of progress is a contribution: a new method, a new understanding, a new dataset or benchmark, a careful reproduction, or an honest negative result. Your first one does not need to be novel in the headline sense. It needs to be real, checkable and finished.

Research is a loop, not a lightbulb

Popular accounts make research sound like a single flash of insight. In practice it is a loop that runs many times: ask a narrow question, find out what is already known, state a claim that could be wrong, run an experiment designed to catch you if it is, and report what happened. The last step matters as much as the first, because a result only counts once someone else can inspect how you got it.

The research loop. Everything in this track trains one of these boxes.

The kinds of contribution

A common beginner mistake is believing only a new algorithm counts. Reviewers and supervisors recognise several kinds of contribution, and some are far more reachable for someone starting out.

Why start with a reproduction
A reproduction forces you through the whole loop on a problem someone else already scoped. It teaches you what papers leave out, gives you an artifact you can show, and often turns up the gap that becomes your own question. Pineau and colleagues built the NeurIPS reproducibility effort partly because so many results were hard to rerun, so a careful rerun is valued, not a consolation prize.

Is it a contribution?

You rerun a published RAG evaluation on the authors' released data, log five seeds, and find the headline gain shrinks by half. You publish the code and a short report.
Reproductions with documented discrepancies are checkable evidence about a claim in the literature. The code and report are what make it count.
You fine-tune a model on your company's private tickets, see better numbers, and describe it in a blog post with no code or data.
A result nobody else can inspect is an anecdote. You would need a public or synthetic dataset to turn the same idea into something checkable.
You build a small benchmark that injects duplicate and stale documents into a public corpus and measures how retrieval degrades. You release the script and the results table.
Benchmarks and controlled analyses are recognised contributions, especially in data-centric work, as long as construction and results are documented.

What a reviewer asks about any contribution

  • What exactly is the claim, in one sentence?
  • What would show the claim is wrong, and did you look for it?
  • Compared with what? A claim of improvement needs a fair baseline.
  • Could someone else rerun this and get the same answer?
  • What are the limits, stated by you rather than discovered by them?

Check your understanding

1. Which is the most reachable first contribution for someone with no publications?
A reproduction is scoped for you, finishes on a deadline and produces checkable evidence, so it is the usual recommendation for a first project.
2. Why does a result obtained on private company data not count as a research contribution on its own?
The test is whether others can verify the claim. Public or synthetic data lets them do that.
