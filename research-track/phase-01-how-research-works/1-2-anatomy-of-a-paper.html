1.2 The anatomy of a paper

Every section of a paper answers one specific question. Once you know the questions, reading and writing both get faster.

TL;DR
A paper is a compressed argument: abstract (the whole claim), introduction (problem, gap, contributions), related work (where it sits), method (what was done), experiments (the evidence), analysis and limitations (honesty) and conclusion. Learn what each section must answer and you can triage any paper in minutes.

The standard shape

Most computer science papers follow the same skeleton, with small variations between machine learning and database venues. Treat it as a checklist of questions the authors owe the reader.

Where the evidence lives: the experiments section is where you check whether the claim is earned.

What each section must answer

Can I state the problem, the approach and the headline result after reading only this? If the abstract is vague, expect the paper to be vague. Note any number it reports, because you will check it later in the results table.

Look for a sentence that starts a gap: existing methods fail to. Then find the contribution list, usually three bullets. Those bullets are the claims the rest of the paper has to support, so keep them in front of you.

This section tells you who the authors consider their neighbours. Missing neighbours are a warning sign, and the citations are a free reading list. Papers cited by several of your target papers are the foundations of the topic.

Ask whether you could reimplement this from the text. Hidden details, unexplained hyperparameters and vague data processing are the places reproductions fail.

Check four things: are the baselines strong and fairly tuned, is more than one dataset used, are results over several seeds with a spread, and do the ablations remove one component at a time?

Good authors write this themselves. A paper with no limitations section is not flawless, it simply left the work to the reviewers. Many venues now ask for limitations explicitly.

Claims and evidence: the table to build

As you read, keep a two column table. In the left column list each claim from the contribution bullets. In the right column write the exact figure, table or experiment that supports it. If a claim has nothing in the right column, you have found a weakness, or a place where you did not read carefully enough.

Flash cards: section to question

Check your understanding

1. Where should you look to find the specific claims a paper must support?
The contribution list states what the authors say they have shown. Everything else is evidence for those claims.
2. A results table shows one run per method with no spread. What is the right reaction?
A single run cannot separate a real gain from noise. The NeurIPS checklist asks authors to report error bars or the equivalent.
3. Why are ablations valuable?
Ablations attribute the improvement. Without them you cannot tell which idea mattered.
