1.3 Venues, preprints and peer review

Where research is published changes how much weight it carries, how fast it appears and what you can realistically target as a beginner.

TL;DR
Preprints (arXiv) are fast and unreviewed. Conferences are the main venue in computer science and review by deadline. Journals are slower and revise. Workshops are the usual first target: smaller, quicker and often non-archival. Read a venue's call for papers before you plan around it, because deadlines and rules change every year.

The four places work appears

In machine learning the big general venues include NeurIPS, ICML and ICLR, and in natural language processing ACL and EMNLP. For database and data management work the main venues include SIGMOD, VLDB, ICDE and CIDR. NeurIPS also has a Datasets and Benchmarks track, which is relevant to benchmark style projects. Workshops attached to these conferences, such as ones on data management for machine learning, are often the most approachable. Always confirm current names, deadlines and scope on the venue's own call for papers, since I have not verified this year's details.

Non-archival workshops
Some workshops are explicitly non-archival, meaning your paper is not formally published in proceedings and you can still send the work to a full conference later. Others are archival and count as prior publication. The call for papers says which. Read it before you submit anything.

How peer review works, in practice

From submission to decision

1. Submit

You upload the paper by the deadline, usually anonymised so reviewers do not see your name. Many venues also ask you to fill in a reproducibility or ethics checklist.

2. Assignment

An area chair assigns the paper to usually three reviewers who work in related areas. They are volunteers with their own deadlines, which is why reviews can be short or uneven.

3. Reviews

Reviewers summarise the paper, list strengths and weaknesses, and give a score. Weaknesses that recur across reviewers are the ones to take seriously.

4. Rebuttal

Many conferences let you respond once. The effective rebuttal answers each concrete criticism with evidence, and does not argue about tone.

5. Decision

Accept, reject or revise. A rejection is normal, even for strong work. The useful move is to fix the real weaknesses and resubmit elsewhere, not to treat the score as a verdict on you.

Preprints and priority

Posting to arXiv lets people cite and build on your work immediately and puts a date stamp on your idea. arXiv requires an endorsement before your first submission and before submitting to a new category, so plan for that rather than discovering it on submission day. Check each venue's policy on preprints, because a few restrict posting during review, though most machine learning venues allow it.

Where would you send it?

You finished a reproduction with one extension. It is a solid two page report with a public repo. You want feedback and a public record before you apply to programmes.
A preprint makes the work citable straight away, and a workshop gives expert feedback without the competition of a main track.
A workshop call says submissions are archival. You also plan to send the same paper to a full conference next month.
Dual submission policies differ. Violating them can get both submissions rejected, so read the call for papers.

Check your understanding

1. Which venue type is usually the most realistic first target for a newcomer?
Workshops are smaller, quicker and more approachable, and many are non-archival.
2. What does a non-archival workshop mean for your paper?
Non-archival means no formal proceedings. Always confirm in the call for papers.
3. What is the best response to a harsh reviewer comment in a rebuttal?
Reviewers and area chairs respond to evidence. Tone arguments rarely change a decision.
