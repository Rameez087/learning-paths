3.2 Statistics for experiments

Every claim of improvement is a statistical claim. This page lets you see how often a single lucky run misleads, and how many seeds it takes to be sure.

TL;DR
Training is random, so one run of each method is one draw from a distribution. Report the mean and spread over several seeds (3 is the floor, 5 or more is better), add a confidence interval, and only claim a gain when the intervals clearly separate or a suitable test agrees. Whatever you report, say how many seeds you used.

Try it: how many seeds do you need?

Method B is truly better than A by the gap you set. Each seed adds random noise. Change the seeds, noise and gap, then press the button to draw a fresh set of runs. Watch how often a small true gap hides inside the intervals, and how a single run can even show the wrong winner.

These numbers are simulated from a normal distribution, so the exact values mean nothing. The point is the pattern: more seeds shrink the interval, and a true gap smaller than the noise needs many seeds to show.

What to report

ItemWhyMinimum
Number of seedsLets readers judge the evidence3, ideally 5 or more
Mean and standard deviationShows the spread across seedsAlways
Confidence interval or standard errorShows how precisely the mean is knownAlways for comparisons
A significance testBacks a claim of differenceWhen claiming one method beats another
Same data splits and tuning budgetKeeps the comparison fairAlways
Common traps
Tuning your new method hard while leaving the baseline at defaults is the quietest way to fake a gain. Picking the best seed after the fact does the same. Giving every method the same tuning budget and reporting all seeds avoids both. The NeurIPS checklist asks authors whether they report error bars and how they were computed.

Check your understanding

1. Why can one run of each method mislead?
Run to run variance can be as large as the gap you are claiming, which is why several seeds are needed.
2. Two methods have overlapping 95% intervals. What is the careful conclusion?
Overlap means the data do not separate the methods. More seeds narrow the intervals.
3. Which practice most clearly makes a comparison unfair?
Unequal tuning effort inflates the apparent gain. Give each method a comparable budget.

Sources: NeurIPS Paper Checklist; Improving Reproducibility in ML Research (Pineau et al.); Welch's t-test, overview
