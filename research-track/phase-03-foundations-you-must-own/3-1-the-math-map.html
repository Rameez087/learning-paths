3.1 The math map

You do not need all of mathematics. You need the specific pieces that appear in method sections, and a way to tell when you are missing one.

TL;DR
Four areas cover most machine learning papers: linear algebra (vectors, matrices, SVD), calculus (gradients, the chain rule), probability and statistics (likelihood, Bayes, tests) and optimisation (gradient descent and its variants). For each, aim to be able to do a specific task, not just recognise the words.

What to be able to do

Four areas, four tests

Be able to: multiply matrices and say what the shapes mean; explain what an eigenvector is; describe what SVD does and why it is used for compression and embeddings; write a dense layer as a matrix product.

Why it matters: embeddings, attention and every neural network layer are matrix operations.

Be able to: take a gradient of a simple function; apply the chain rule through a composition; explain backpropagation as the chain rule applied systematically.

Why it matters: training is following gradients, and you will read papers that derive new ones.

Be able to: define likelihood and maximum likelihood estimation; apply Bayes rule; explain a confidence interval and a hypothesis test; say what a p value is and what it is not.

Why it matters: every claim of improvement is a statistical claim. See 3.2.

Be able to: explain gradient descent and what the learning rate changes; describe momentum and Adam at a high level; explain regularisation as a penalty on complexity.

Why it matters: training instability is one of the most common reasons reproductions disagree with papers.

A practical rule
When a method section loses you, find the single symbol or step you cannot read and learn only that. Collecting such gaps in your reading log (2.3) gives you a personal syllabus far more efficient than studying maths in the abstract.

Which piece is missing?

A paper writes the update as theta minus eta times the gradient of the loss. You do not know what eta controls.
This is the gradient descent update rule. Eta, often alpha, is the learning rate.
A paper reports that method A beat B by 0.4 points and calls it significant, but gives only one run each.
Whether a difference is larger than run to run noise is a statistical question, taken up in 3.2.
