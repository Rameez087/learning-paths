2.1 The three-pass method

Reading a paper from the first word to the last is the slowest way to understand it. Keshav's method gets you the gist in minutes and the depth only when it is worth it.

TL;DR
Read every paper in three passes. Pass one (5 to 10 minutes) decides whether the paper deserves more time. Pass two (up to an hour) lets you explain the paper and its evidence while skipping proofs. Pass three (hours) rebuilds the argument as if you were the author. Most papers should only ever get pass one.

Why three passes

Keshav's short paper on reading is the most widely cited advice on the subject in computer science. Its central idea is that reading is a funnel. You cannot afford a deep read of everything, so each pass is a filter that decides whether the next one is worth your time. It also notes that most reviewers give a paper only one pass, which is a useful reminder of what your own abstract and introduction have to carry.

Each pass is a filter. Most papers should stop after the first.

Walk through the passes

Pass 1: the bird's eye view

Read the title, abstract and introduction. Read the section and subsection headings and ignore everything else. Read the conclusions. Glance at the references and tick the ones you already know.

You should now be able to answer the five Cs: Category (what type of paper is this), Context (which other papers and theory it relates to), Correctness (do the assumptions look valid), Contributions (what the main ones are) and Clarity (is it well written).

Pass 2: the content, not the details

Read with care but skip proofs. Study the figures, diagrams and tables closely. Check that axes are labelled, that results have error bars and that comparisons are fair. Mark references you have not read and might read next.

After this pass you should be able to summarise the paper and its main evidence to someone else.

Pass 3: virtual re-implementation

Recreate the paper in your head: make the same assumptions and work out the method yourself, then compare it with the paper. This is how you find hidden assumptions and the places where the authors glossed over a difficulty.

Do this only for the few papers that matter to your project, such as the one you will reproduce.

Pass 1 drill: the five Cs

Pick any paper and try this against the clock. If you cannot tick all five in ten minutes, that tells you something about the paper or about your background in the area.

Budget your reading
Reading three to five recent papers on one topic is usually enough to map a subfield. For each, do pass one. Choose one or two for pass two. Choose a single paper for pass three. That ratio keeps you from drowning in PDFs.

Check your understanding

1. What should you be able to answer after the first pass?
Pass one is a triage. It gives you enough to decide whether to continue, not enough to reproduce the work.
2. When is a full third pass worth the hours?
Pass three is expensive. It pays off when you need to reimplement or critique the work in depth.
3. Why does Keshav's observation that reviewers often make one pass matter for your own writing?
If a reader only gives you one pass, the title, abstract, headings and conclusions need to deliver the gist.
