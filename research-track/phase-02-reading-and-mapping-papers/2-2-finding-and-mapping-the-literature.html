2.2 Finding and mapping the literature

You cannot find a good question without knowing what has already been asked. A literature map built in an afternoon beats weeks of scattered reading.

TL;DR
Start from one recent, well cited paper or a survey, then chain backward through its references and forward through who cites it. Use Google Scholar, Semantic Scholar, DBLP and arXiv together. Stop when new papers keep citing the same ones. Check that your exact question is not already answered before you invest weeks.

Citation chaining

Pick a seed paper, ideally a recent survey or a paper everyone in the area cites. Go backward by reading its related work and reference list. Go forward by opening its Cited by list and sorting by date to find the newest work. Papers that appear in several reference lists are the foundations. Papers that cite the seed recently show where the field is moving.

Build a literature map in an afternoon

1. Pick the seed

Search your topic plus the word survey, or pick the most cited recent paper in the area. Write the topic in one sentence first so you can tell which papers are off topic.

2. Collect 20 titles

From the seed's references and citations, collect about twenty titles. Skim titles and abstracts only. Put each into a reference manager such as Zotero, which is free.

3. Cluster

Sort the papers into three to five groups by approach, such as methods, benchmarks and analyses. A cluster with only old papers may be a closed question. A cluster of very recent papers is where the action is.

4. Find the shared citations

Note which earlier papers appear in many of the lists. Read those first. They define the vocabulary of the field.

5. Check novelty

Search for your exact question with several phrasings on Scholar and arXiv. If someone has answered it, you have found either a reproduction target or a reason to refine the question.

Which move fits?

You found one excellent recent paper and want to know what came after it.
Forward chaining through citations shows follow-up and competing work, which the reference list cannot.
You need the standard background papers for a subfield you do not know.
Papers repeated across reference lists are the foundations. Any AI generated list must be verified, because fabricated references are a known failure.

Check your understanding

1. What is backward citation chaining?
Backward means older work the paper cites. Forward means newer work that cites the paper.
2. Why check novelty before investing weeks in a project?
A quick search with several phrasings can save weeks and sometimes reveals the better version of your question.
3. Which signal suggests a paper is a foundation of a subfield?
Repeated citation across independent papers is a good proxy for foundational status.
